**Samāna**
Meaning: "equalized"
See: [Mandukya Upanishad (Verse 10)](mandukyopanisat.md)

## Meaning

Equal, impartial, or treated with equal regard by all; denoting one who attains equanimity and is viewed without enmity by friends and foes alike.

## Definition

In the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*, *samāna* (समान) functions as a predicate adjective within the clause *samānaś ca bhavati* (समानश्च भवति, "and he becomes equal" or "he is treated equally by all"), occurring in the tenth mantra. It describes an experiential and interpersonal fruit (*phala*) achieved by an aspirant who meditates upon and realizes the identity between *Taijasa* (the subtle self presiding over the dream state) and the phoneme *U* (*ukāra*), the second measure (*mātrā*) of the sacred syllable *AUM*. 

Literally, *samāna* signifies "equal," "same," "uniform," or "impartial." Within its specific Upaniṣadic setting, it denotes both internal and external dimensions of harmony: subjectively, the knower attains an attitude of equanimity, poise, and impartiality; objectively and socially, the knower is regarded with equal goodwill by all persons, remaining free from the rivalry, malice, or hostility of others. 

This adjectival usage must be distinguished from the homophonous masculine noun *samāna* (समान), which designates one of the five vital physiological functions (*pañcaprāṇa*) responsible for digestion and internal balancing of bodily nourishment. In the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*, the term is strictly the qualitative adjective denoting equality and impartiality.

## Etymology

The adjective *samāna* (समान) is formed from the pronominal/adjectival base *sama* (सम, meaning "same," "equal," "level," or "impartial") combined with the secondary suffix *-na* (or formative suffix *-āna*), denoting likeness, identity, or parity. 

In the textual passage *samānaś ca bhavati* (*Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*, Mantra 10):
- **Base**: *samāna* (stem), adjective.
- **Gender, Number, Case**: Masculine, singular, nominative (*samānaḥ*, समानः), agreeing with the implied subject *yaḥ* (यः, "he who [knows thus]").
- **Sandhi Modification**: The original nominative form *samānaḥ* combines with the enclitic conjunction *ca* (च, "and"). Under standard rules of Sanskrit external visarga-sandhi, an unvoiced visarga followed by a palatal stop (*c*) assimilates to the voiceless palatal sibilant *ś* (श्), yielding the conjunct form *samānaś ca* (समानश्च).
- **Verbal Relation**: It serves as the complement to the finite verb *bhavati* (भवति, third-person singular present indicative active of the root √*bhū*, "to be" or "to become").

This adjective is morphologically distinct from the masculine noun *sam-āná* (vital breath), which is derived from the verbal prefix *sam* (सम्, "together") prefixed to the root *an* (अन्, "to breathe") with the primary agentive suffix *-a* (*ghañ* / *ac*).

## Samāna in the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad

The term occurs in Mantra 10 of the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*:

> स्वप्नस्थानस्तैजस उकारो द्वितीया मात्रोत्कर्षादुभयत्वाद्वोत्कर्षति ह वै ज्ञानसंततिं समानश्च भवति नास्याब्रह्मवित्कुले भवति य एवं वेद ॥ १० ॥
> 
> *svapnasthānas taijasa ukāro dvitīyā mātrotkarṣād ubhayatvād votkarṣati ha vai jñānasantatiṃ samānaś ca bhavati nāsya abrahmavit kule bhavati ya evaṃ veda // 10 //*
> 
> "Taijasa, whose sphere of activity is the dream state, is *U*, the second letter [of *AUM*], on account of superiority [*utkarṣa*] or on account of intermediateness [*ubhayatva*]. He who knows this exalts the continuum of knowledge, becomes equal / is treated equally by all [*samānaś ca bhavati*], and in his family no one is born who is not a knower of Brahman—[thus it is for him] who knows this."

### Surrounding Context and Grammatical Function
The *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* correlates the four quarters (*pādas*) of the Self (*Ātman*) with the measures (*mātrās*) of *AUM* (Mantras 8–12). Mantra 9 equates *Vaiśvānara* (the waking self) with the letter *A* on the grounds of pervasiveness (*āpti*) and precedence (*ādimattva*), granting as its fruit that the knower obtains all desires and becomes foremost (*ādiś ca bhavati*). 

Mantra 10 turns to the second quarter, *Taijasa*, and identifies it with the second measure, the vowel *U* (*ukāra*). The Upaniṣad offers two structural grounds for this equation:
1. *Utkarṣāt* (from superiority/elevation): Sequentially and acoustically, *U* appears elevated after *A*; epistemologically, *Taijasa* cognizes subtle, internal mental impressions (*vāsanās*), which represent an inward advance over the gross external perceptions of *Vaiśvānara*.
2. *Ubhayatvāt* (from intermediateness / being intermediate): The letter *U* stands in the middle between *A* and *M*; correspondingly, the dream consciousness (*Taijasa*) occupies an intermediate position between waking (*Vaiśvānara*) and dreamless sleep (*Prājña*).

Following this analysis, the text proclaims a three-part *phala-śruti* (declaration of meditative reward) for the practitioner who realizes this identity:
1. *Utkarṣati ha vai jñānasantatim*: his stream of knowledge increases and exalts.
2. *Samānaś ca bhavati*: he becomes equal or is treated with equality by all.
3. *Nāsya abrahmavit kule bhavati*: no one lacking knowledge of Brahman is born in his lineage.

### Distinction Between Textual Layers

#### The Upaniṣadic Text
The Upaniṣadic prose formulates *samānaś ca bhavati* as a direct consequence of understanding intermediateness (*ubhayatva*) and elevation (*utkarṣa*). It marks the transition of the meditator from external dispersion toward non-dual balance.

#### Gauḍapāda's Kārikā
In the *Āgama Prakaraṇa*, Gauḍapāda restates and analyzes the rationale of Mantra 10 in Kārikā 1.20:
> उत्कर्षात्संप्रतिपत्तिरुकारस्योभयत्वतः ।  
> *utkarṣāt sampratipattir ukārasyobhayatvataḥ |*  
> "The clear ground of realizing Taijasa as of the same nature as U is the common feature of 'superiority'; similarly another plain reason of such identity is being in 'the middle'."

Gauḍapāda focuses his verse commentary on the philosophical justification for the correspondence (*sāmya* / common features) rather than repeating the full *phala-śruti* phrase. In Kārikā 1.22, Gauḍapāda summarizes the fruit of comprehending these commonalities across all three states, noting that one who understands these relationships with certainty is adored and respected by all beings (*sarvabhūtaiḥ pūjyaḥ*), echoing the exalted, harmonious status signified by *samāna*.

#### Śaṅkara’s Commentary
In his commentary (*Bhāṣya*) on Mantra 10, Śaṅkarācārya explains the contextual meaning of *samānaś ca bhavati* specifically in terms of freedom from malice and mutual enmity:
> "He is regarded in the same way by all, i.e., his enemies, like his friends, do not envy [or injure] him."

Śaṅkara glosses *samānaḥ* as being an object of equal, non-hostile regard across opposing groups (*mitra-pakṣa* and *ari-pakṣa*). Because the meditator has understood the intermediate nature of subtle consciousness, which bridges and reconciles outer and inner divisions, the empirical polarities of friend and foe subside into uniform impartiality.

### Philosophical Significance in Advaita Vedānta
In Advaita Vedānta, the dream state demonstrated by *Taijasa* reveals that experienced phenomena are essentially mental appearances projected upon consciousness. Meditating on *Taijasa* as the intermediate syllable *U* loosens attachment to gross objectivity and leads the mind toward the stillness of *Prājña* and ultimately the non-dual transcendence of *Turīya*. The realization of *samāna* reflects the dawn of *samadarśana* (equal vision) and *samatva* (equanimity), wherein the knower transcends personal partiality and abides in the flawless, impartial reality of Brahman.

In Karl H. Potter's survey of early Vedāntic literature, *samāna* constitutes one of the fivefold functional modifications of the cosmic vital energy (*prāṇa*) operating within the individual subtle body (*liṅgaśarīra*). Together with the five organs of action (*karmendriya*), the five vital airs form the vital sheath (*prāṇamayakośa*).

Vedāntic anatomical classification places *samāna* specifically at the navel (*nābhi*). Its doctrinal function is defined as *samīkaraṇa*—literally "making equal" or "equalizing". It digests food, breaks down nourishment, and distributes the resulting essence equally through the vascular and subtle channels (*nāḍī*) across all bodily members. At the same time, Śaṅkarācārya and his commentators emphasize the non-ultimacy of *samāna*: the vital breaths are material, nonconscious products born of the elements and nescience (*avidyā*). The true Self (*ātman*) is utterly transcendent, witness to the subtle body, and eternally free from life-breath, hunger, and physical assimilation.

## Samāna in Sāṃkhya

In Sanskrit lexicography and grammar, the term *samāna* (समान) possesses two distinct etymological and conceptual derivations. Derived from the root *sama*, it functions as an adjective meaning "same," "equal," "similar," "homogeneous," or "common." When formed from the verbal root *an* ("to breathe") prefixed with *sam* ("together"), it functions as a masculine technical noun (*sam-āna*) designating the assimilative or unifying vital breath (*vāyu* / *prāṇa*) that gathers at the center of the organism. While ordinary usage frequently employs *samāna* in the adjectival sense of equivalence or uniformity, classical Indian philosophical traditions assign it a foundational technical role within bodily physiology, psychology, and subtle energy systems.

In classical Sāṃkhya, *samāna* is enumerated as one of the five vital airs (*pañcavāyu* or *pañcaprāṇa*: *prāṇa*, *apāna*, *samāna*, *udāna*, and *vyāna*). In the *Sāṅkhyakārikā* (kārikā 29) of Īśvarakṛṣṇa, these five airs are defined as the collective, common function (*sāmānya-karaṇa-vṛtti*) of the three internal instruments (*antaḥkaraṇa*): the intellect (*buddhi* / *mahat*), egoity (*ahaṅkāra*), and the mind (*manas*). While each internal organ possesses an uncommon or specific function (*asāmānyā vṛttiḥ* / *svalakṣaṇya*)—resolution or ascertainment (*adhyavasāya*) for *buddhi*, self-assertion or identification (*abhimāna*) for *ahaṅkāra*, and intention or synthesis (*saṅkalpa*) for *manas*—their joint, harmonious activity sustains living embodiment through the fivefold breath. Vācaspati Miśra’s *Tattvakaumudī* explains that these vital airs are the very life of the internal organs, enduring as long as the organs maintain embodiment and collapsing when they withdraw.

Within Sāṃkhya bodily mechanics, *samāna* is specifically located in the heart, the navel, and all the bodily joints (*hṛdi nābhyāṃ sarvasandhiṣu*). Larson and Bhattacharya classify *samāna* as the "digestion" or "nutrient breath," operating primarily in the region between the navel and the heart to assimilate ingested nourishment and distribute essential fluids and nutrients equally to every part of the physical organism. Classical expositors also contrast its internal and external dimensions: internally, *samāna* sustains the metabolic digestion of food and fuel; externally, it supports the embodied agent's capacity for coherent social interaction and bodily balance.

Doctrinal commentaries within Sāṃkhya also address internal debates regarding the ontological nature of the *vāyus*. In the *Yuktidīpikā* and later surveys, the five breaths are shown to proceed from the internal organs—specifically tracing back to egoity (*ahaṅkāra*) under the influence of the *rajas* and *tamas* constituents (*guṇas*). Furthermore, commentators such as Bālārāma Udāsīna (*Cittaprasādinī*) debated whether the vital airs constitute physical air or operations of the instruments metaphorically termed "air" due to their motility. Bālārāma observes that vital airs continue their vitalizing activities uninterrupted even during deep dreamless sleep (*suṣupti*), when external senses cease functioning, confirming that *samāna* and its companion breaths are the exclusive functional modifications of the three internal capacities.

## Samāna in Yoga

In the Pātañjala Yoga tradition, *samāna* is integrated into the practical psychology and psychosomatic metaphysics of the *Yogasūtras*. Patañjali addresses *samāna* directly in the context of the extraordinary attainments (*siddhis*) arising from the mastery of *saṃyama*—the threefold meditative synthesis of concentration (*dhāraṇā*), absorption (*dhyāna*), and integration (*samādhi*) (YS III.4).

In *Yogasūtra* III.41, Patañjali states: *samāna-jayāj jvalanam* ("By mastery over *samāna*, blazing occurs"). In modern scholarship and traditional exposition, *samāna* is identified as the vital current operating in the *prāṇamaya kośa* (vital sheath) around the solar plexus and navel center (*nābhi-cakra*). I. K. Taimni explains that *samāna* directly governs the digestive fire (*jaṭharāgni*) and the metabolic processing of food. By focusing *saṃyama* upon *samāna*, the practitioner obtains conscious mastery over glandular and digestive secretions, allowing the yogin to intensify internal heat and produce an outward effulgence or radiant aura (*jvalana*) surrounding the physical frame. Chip Hartranft similarly translates the aphorism as gaining radiance through the mastery of the vital energy flow coursing through the solar plexus.

## Samāna in Tantra and Śākta-Yoga

In Tantric and Śākta-Yoga texts, such as the *Ṣaṭ-Cakra-Nīrūpaṇa* and the *Mahānirvāṇa Tantra* translated and analyzed by Arthur Avalon, *samāna* is explained as a localized manifestation of the cosmic feminine life-power (*Kuṇḍalinī* / *Prāṇa-devatā*). The *Mahānirvāṇa Tantra* describes the five primary *vāyus* with distinctive colors, assigning a "milky" appearance to *samāna* and stating that it kindles the bodily fire, "conducting equally the food, etc., throughout the body".

Within the subtle cakra system, *samāna* is localized in the abdominal region centered at the *Maṇipūra cakra* (the ten-petaled lotus at the navel). The *Maṇipūra* is the seat of the fire element (*tejas tattva*), marked by the red seed-syllable *Raṃ*. Avalon characterizes *samāna* as the functional force of "Assimilation," distinguishing it from Appropriation (*prāṇa*), Rejection (*apāna*), Distribution (*vyāna*), and Utterance (*udāna*). In both Śākta-Yoga and Indo-Tibetan esoteric teachings, *samāna* acts as the unifying metabolic current that supports equilibrium among the bodily humors (*doṣas*) and bodily tissues (*dhātus*), maintaining physical stability during spiritual practice.

## Samāna in Nyāya

In the Nyāya tradition, the term *samāna* shifts from physiological and subtle-breath mechanics to logical and epistemological analysis. In Gautama’s *Nyāyasūtra* (NS 1.1.23), the term appears in the foundational compound *samānānekadharmopapatteḥ*, which articulates the foremost cause of doubt (*saṃśaya*).

Commenting on this sūtra, Vātsyāyana’s *Nyāyasūtrabhāṣya* and Uddyotakara’s *Nyāyavārttika* explain that *samāna* here signifies "common" (*sādhāraṇa*) or "similar" (*sādṛśya*). Uddyotakara clarifies that *samāna* does not refer to an abstract universal (*sāmānya*) inhering in two distinct entities, but to an observed shared property. For example, when an observer in dim light notices upright stature (*ūrdhvatva*)—a feature common (*samāna*) both to a human being and to a post—the apprehension of this common quality without the perception of specific distinguishing marks (*viśeṣa*) induces a wavering mental oscillation (*vimarśa*) between the two possibilities. Uddyotakara adds that a character is termed *samāna* when it subsists in things that are homogeneous (*sajātīya*) as well as heterogeneous (*vijātīya*) to the object in question, thereby preventing immediate inferential certainty.
