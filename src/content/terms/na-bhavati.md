**Na Bhavati** 
Meaning: "not born"
See: [Mandukya Upanishad (Verses 10)](mandukyopanisat.md)

## Meaning

The phrase *na bhavati* (न भवति) means 'does not become' or 'is not', denoting non-origination and non-existence in context.

---

## Definition

The Sanskrit expression *na bhavati* (न भवति) is a two-word negative verbal phrase comprising the negative particle *na* (न) and the third-person singular present indicative active verb form *bhavati* (भवति). In Sanskrit syntax, the particle *na* serves as a clause-level negative adverb that directly modifies and negates the action, state, or transformation denoted by the verb *bhavati*.

Literally, *na bhavati* translates to "does not become", "does not exist", "is not", or "does not come into being". Idiomatically, in philosophical discourses, the expression denotes absolute non-existence, the denial of change, or the negation of causal origination. In the context-dependent framework of Vedāntic ontology and the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* tradition, *na bhavati* functions as an explicit denial of modification (*vikāra*) or birth (*jāti*): it indicates that ultimate reality (*Brahman* or *Turīya*) never undergoes real transformation, and that phenomenal duality does not truly exist or come into being.

---

## Etymology

The phrase *na bhavati* (न भवति) is formed from two distinct grammatical constituents:

1. **na** (न): An uninflected negative particle (*avyaya*) functioning as the standard particle of negation in Sanskrit grammar. Placed before a finite verb, it negates the verbal predicate directly.
2. **bhavati** (भवति): The third-person singular present indicative active (*prathama-puruṣa eka-vacana parasmaipada*) form of the verbal root *bhū* (भू), belonging to the first conjugational class (*bhvādi-gaṇa*). The verbal root *bhū* signifies "to be", "to become", "to exist", or "to arise". In its morphological formation, the root *bhū* takes the *śap* (अ) class-infix, yielding *guṇa* strengthening of the root vowel (*bhū* \\(\rightarrow\\) *bho* + *a* + *ti* \\(\rightarrow\\) *bhavati*).

In sandhi combinations, *na* and *bhavati* remain syntactically distinct without euphonic coalition. The phrase represents a standard negative verbal construction without nominal compounding (*samāsa*).

## Na bhavati in the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad

The *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*, preserved within the *Atharva Veda*, is the shortest of the principal Upaniṣads, consisting of twelve prose mantras. The text analyzes human experience across three states of consciousness—waking (*jāgrat* / *Viśva*), dream (*svapna* / *Taijasa*), and deep sleep (*suṣupti* / *Prājña*)—and correlates them with the three sound-matras of *AUM* (A, U, M). The Upaniṣad culminates in revealing the transcendental Fourth (*Turīya*), which is partless (*amātra*), non-dual (*advaita*), and characterized by the total cessation of phenomenal appearance (*prapañcopaśama*).

In the core text of the Upaniṣad (notably Mantra 7), negation is primarily articulated through nominal prefixes (*na-* / *a-*) to define *Turīya* via the apophatic method of *neti neti* ("not this, not this"). Within this textual setting, the verbal negation *na bhavati* conveys the foundational ontological assertion that in the non-dual reality of *Turīya*, dualistic phenomena do not occur, exist, or undergo transformation.

### Gauḍapāda's Kārikā and Śaṅkara's Commentary
Gauḍapāda, in his *Māṇḍūkya Kārikā* (also titled *Āgamaśāstra*), systematically expands the Upaniṣad's apophatic statements into the formal doctrine of *ajātivāda* (the theory of absolute non-origination). Gauḍapāda utilizes the verbal root *bhū* and its negative forms (such as *na bhavati*, *na jāyate*, and *ajāti*) to demonstrate that no entity ever comes into existence. In Chapter III (*Advaita Prakaraṇa*) and Chapter IV (*Alātāśānti Prakaraṇa*), Gauḍapāda asserts that an immortal, birthless entity cannot logically become mortal or undergo real change (*na bhavati*).

In his *Māṇḍūkyopaniṣad-bhāṣya*, Śaṅkara corroborates Gauḍapāda's formulation, maintaining that the apparent origination, manifestation, and destruction of the universe are illusory projections (*māyā*) superimposed on *Brahman* through nescience (*avidyā*). Śaṅkara explains that just as an imagined snake does not actually exist in a rope, world-duality does not come into being (*na bhavati*) in the supreme Self. Thus, while the Upaniṣad presents nominal negations to isolate *Turīya*, later Advaita commentators deploy the verbal expression *na bhavati* to refute causal origination (*kāraṇa-kārya-bhāva*) and affirm the immutable nature of non-dual Consciousness.
