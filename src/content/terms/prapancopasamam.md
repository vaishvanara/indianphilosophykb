Meaning: "the negation of all relative existence;" the total cessation or stillness of all phenomenal multiplicity in the non-dual Self (*Turīya*).

See: [Mandukya Upanishad (Verses 7, 12)](mandukyopanisat.md)

---

## Definition

The Sanskrit compound prapañcopaśamaṃ (Sanskrit: प्रपञ्चोपशमं) is an adjectival epithet formed from two primary nominal components: prapañca (प्रपञ्च) and upaśama (उपशम). 

* ***Prapañca* (प्रपञ्च)** literally signifies expansion, manifestation, multiformity, or the manifold phenomenal world consisting of names, forms, and empirical experiences.
* ***Upaśama* (उपशम)** denotes quietude, pacification, abatement, or complete cessation and stilling.

Combined, *prapañcopaśama* literally means "the stilling or cessation of phenomenal manifestation". Contextually, as used in verse 7 of the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*, *prapañcopaśamaṃ* functions as a descriptive attribute of *Turīya* (the "Fourth" state of consciousness or pure *Ātman*). It conveys that in the supreme reality of the Self, the entire empirical universe of duality, encompassing waking, dream, and deep-sleep experiences, ceases to exist as an independent entity. Epistemologically and ontologically, this does not imply a physical destruction of external objects, but rather the dissolution of world-delusion upon realizing the non-dual substratum (*advaita*).

## Etymology

The Sanskrit term *prapañcopaśamaṃ* exhibits a precise compound word formation (*samāsa*) and phonological junction (*sandhi*):

### Sandhi formation

The compound base is produced by *guṇa-sandhi* joining the final short vowel *-a* of *prapañca* with the initial short vowel *u-* of *upaśama* (*a* + *u* = *o*), yielding *prapañcopaśama*.

### Constituent derivations

- ***Prapañca* (m.):** Derived from the prefix *pra*- (forth, intensely) combined with the root *pañc* (to spread, expand) or nominal expansion, signifying the multiform development or expanse of cosmic phenomena.
- ***Upaśama* (m.):** Derived from the prefix *upa*- (towards, near) combined with the verbal root *śam* (to become calm, quiet, cease), denoting pacification, alleviation, or complete disappearance.

### Compound classification (samāsa)

- **Genitive *tatpuruṣa* (*saṣṭhī-tatpuruṣa*):** Analyzed in its expanded form (*vigraha-vākya*) as *prapañcasya upaśamaḥ* ("the cessation of phenomenal multiplicity").
- **Adjectival *bahuvrīhi*:** When functioning as a modifier of *Turīya* or Ātman, it is resolved as *prapañcaḥ upaśāmyati yasmin saḥ* ("that in which all phenomenal multiplicity is stilled").

### Inflection

The neuter nominative singular inflection *-m* (*prapañcopaśamaṃ*) reflects syntactic agreement with the implied neuter subject brahma or the unconditioned aspects of *Turīya* described in the seventh verse.

## Prapañcopaśamaṃ in the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad

The expression *prapañcopaśamaṃ* occurs directly in verse 7 of the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*. The verse defines the nature of *Turīya*, the ultimate reality underlying the three transient states of human experience:

> *nāntaḥprajñaṃ na bahiṣprajñaṃ nobhayataḥprajñaṃ na prajñānaghanaṃ na prajñaṃ nāprajñam | adṛṣṭam avyavahāryam agrāhyam alakṣaṇam acintyam avyapadeśyam ekātmapratyayasāraṃ prapañcopaśamaṃ śāntaṃ śivam advaitaṃ caturthaṃ manyante sa ātmā sa vijñeyaḥ* ||
> 
> "Not consciousness inward, not consciousness outward, not consciousness of both, not a mass of consciousness, not consciousness, and not unconsciousness. The fourth (*Turīya*) is considered as invisible, unrelated, ungraspable, indefinable, inconceivable, ineffable, the Essence of the consciousness of Selfhood and Oneness, the negation of all relative existence, peaceful, auspicious, and non-dual; that is the Self; that is to be realized."

### Grammatical function and context

Within verse 7, *prapañcopaśamaṃ* serves as a vital negative qualifying attribute (*viśeṣaṇa*) modifying the Fourth (*caturtha* / *Turīya*). The *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* structures *Ātman* into four quarters (*pāda*): 

1. ***Viśva*** (waking state, *jāgarita-sthāna*), experiencing gross objects;
2. ***Taijasa*** (dream state, *svapna-sthāna*), experiencing subtle impressions;
3. ***Prājña*** (deep sleep state, *suṣupti-sthāna*), unified mass of consciousness without duality;
4. ***Turīya*** (the Fourth), the non-dual, unconditioned Reality.

The surrounding text systematically negates all empirical attributes: *Turīya* is neither conscious of the internal dream world (*na antaḥ-prajñam*), nor of the external waking world (*na bahiṣ-prajñam*), nor of both (*na ubhayataḥ-prajñam*), nor a dormant mass of consciousness (*na prajñāna-ghanam*). It is invisible (*adṛṣya*), unrelated (*avyavahāryam*), incomprehensible (*agrāhyam*), uninferable (*alakṣaṇam*), unthinkable (*acintyam*), and indescribable (*avyapadeśyam*). Following these apophatic negations, *prapañcopaśamaṃ* declares that in this state, all world-manifestation ceases completely. It is complemented by positive ontological indicators: tranquility (*śāntam*), auspiciousness (*śivam*), and non-duality (*advaitam*).

### Gauḍapāda's Kārikā and Śaṅkara's Bhāṣya

* **Upaniṣadic text vs. Gauḍapāda's *Kārikā***: The Upanishadic verse concisely states the ultimate truth (*paramārtha*). Gauḍapāda, in his *Kārikā* (particularly Books 1 and 2), expands this by demonstrating through logic (*upapatti*) that the manifold world (*prapañca*) is illusory (*vaitathya*) like a dream or a rope mistaken for a snake. In *Kārikā* 1.18 and 2.35, Gauḍapāda establishes that once the non-dual Reality is known, the imagined duality (*dvaita*) of master, student, and scripture disappears, leaving only *prapañcopaśama*.
* **Śaṅkarācārya's *Bhāṣya***: Śaṅkara explains that *prapañcopaśamaṃ* does not mean that a real world existed previously and was later destroyed. Rather, just as the illusory snake vanishes when the rope is recognized for what it is, the world of duality (*prapañca*) is recognized as having never been produced separately from *Brahman*. *Turīya* is the eternal, unchanging substratum (*adhiṣṭhāna*) free from all superimpositions (*adhyāsa*).

## Interpretations and disputes

### Sublation vs. physical annihilation

- **Advaita Vedānta view**: Traditional Advaitins (Gauḍapāda, Śaṅkara, Ānandagiri) maintain that *prapañcopaśama* represents sublation through knowledge (*bādha*) rather than physical destruction (*pralaya*). The world is an illusory projection (*māyā* / *kalpanā*); hence, its cessation means the termination of ignorance (*avidyā*) and false attribution.
- **Dualist and qualified non-dualist views**: Other schools of Vedānta (such as Madhva's Dvaita and Rāmānuja's Viśiṣṭādvaita) interpret the passage differently, rejecting absolute illusionism. They view *prapañca* as real and regard its stilling in deep sleep or liberation as absorption into God or cessation of worldly suffering, rather than the non-existence of the universe.

### Textual authority and composition disputes

- **Traditional view**: Advaita tradition holds the 12 prose verses of the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* to be ancient Vedic *śruti*, with Gauḍapāda’s *Kārikā* serving as a commentarial exposition (*prakaraṇa-grantha*).
- **Modern scholarly debates**: Certain modern scholars (such as V. S. Bhattacharya and S. N. Dasgupta) debated the historical relationship between the prose mantras and Gauḍapāda's text, with some suggesting Buddhist influences (e.g., Madhyamaka or Vijñānavāda terminology). However, Advaita commentators explicitly highlight that Gauḍapāda explicitly refutes Buddhist conclusions at the end of his treatise (*Kārikā* 4.99: *"Naitad Buddhena bhāṣitam"*), holding that the non-dual Self as *prapañcopaśamaṃ* is unique to the Vedantic *śruti*.

### Translation variations

- **Max Müller**: Translates the term as "the cessation of all phenomena" or "exempt from multiformity."
- **Swami Nikhilananda**: Renders it as "the cessation of all phenomena."
- **Arthur A. MacDonell**: Defines the compound components as "abatement/cessation of expansion or multiformity."
