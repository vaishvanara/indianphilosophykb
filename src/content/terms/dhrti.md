# Dhrti

**Dhṛti** (also spelled **Dhriti** or **Dhruti**; Sanskrit: धृति) is a Sanskrit term that translates to "act with determination," "patience," and "firmness," while also denoting "perseverance" and "wearing regularly". Derived from the root *dhr-* (meaning "to bear"), there is no direct equivalent single word in the English language. It represents a subtle psychological and spiritual faculty that drives an individual continuously toward a goal, serving as a source of peace, courage, enthusiasm, and resilience against obstacles. 

In broader Hindu traditions, Dhrti is recognized as one of the ethical restraints (*yamas*), one of the eleven *Rudrāṇīs*, and an essential virtue characterizing *Dharma*. Epics and texts feature Dhriti in various forms: she is said to have been reborn as Madri in the *Mahabharata*, while texts like the *Lakshmi Sahasranama* and *Lalita Sahasranama* invoke the name as an epithet for divine courage, light, and inner fortitude.

---

## Vedic Relevance

In Vedic literature and traditions, Dhrti holds multiple roles:

* **Vedic Metre:** Dhrti is the designation for a specific Vedic metre consisting of a 12.12.8|12.8|12.8 structure, categorized as a variety of the *atichandas*. It also serves as the name of a yoga configuration in the Hindu calendar.
* **Cosmic Order:** Vedic Rishis invoke the Visvedevas, Adityas, and Maruts as collective protectors of the ten *Rtas*, a group that includes Dhrti (representing forbearance) alongside abstract concepts like *Hri* (modesty) and *Kirti* (glory).
* **Ashvamedha Yajna:** Ritual texts such as the *Shatapatha Brahmana*, *Asvalayana-srauta-sutra*, and *Sankhayana-srauta-sutra* detail the performance of *Dhrti Homas*. During the *Ashvamedha* (horse sacrifice), the Adhvaryu priest offered daily offerings to Savitr for the horse's safe daytime movement and executed four *Dhrti Homas* at nightfall to shelter the sacrificial horse. During these nighttime homas, a Kshatriya lute-player (*veenagathi*) sang verses praising the heroic exploits and wars waged by the royal sacrificer.

## Puranic Relevance

According to Puranic literature and indices:
* Dhrti is described as a son of Dharma (who was a son of Brahma) and Vijaya, who married the daughters of Daksha. 
* Genealogically, Dhrtavarta was recorded as the son of Dhrti and the father of Satyakarma. Nandi is noted as a consort of Dhrti, and Niyama as a son.
* Dhrti—personifying courage—is invoked during *Grahabalis* and is referenced in the stutis of the *Devi Bhagavatam*.
* In the framework of Manu's teachings, Dhrti is counted among the ten fundamental values (*sanatana dharma*) required universally by all people, signifying fortitude and the unwavering capacity to uphold noble principles (*sattvic dhrti*).

---

## Psychological and Philosophical Implications

As an inward faculty, Dhrti provides the psychological backbone required to navigate adversity. The *Bhagavad Gita* categorizes fortitude into three distinct types based on the qualities of nature (*gunas*):

1. **Sattvic Dhrti:** Unswerving fortitude sustained through deep concentration (*yoga*), enabling a person to control the mind, life-breaths, and senses while dedicatedly pursuing a noble cause despite all obstacles.
2. **Rajasic Dhrti:** Fortitude driven by desire, displayed temporarily under specific personal circumstances or to achieve egoistic, material goals, which tends to collapse when conditions shift.
3. **Tamasic Dhrti:** Stubbornness, delusion, or blind persistence wherein an individual stubbornly clings to grief, fear, despair, and falsehood.

---

## Yogic Relevance

In the context of classical yoga philosophy, Patanjali's *Yoga Sutra* (II.28) notes that the progressive removal of impurities through the disciplines of yoga kindles inner illumination up to the peak of discriminatory wisdom (*viveka-khyati*). Classical commentators like Vyasa and Vijnanabhikshu list Dhrti (interpreted here as "sustenance" or endurance) among the core components driving this spiritual evolution. 

Here, Dhrti reflects patience, adherence to moral and religious duties (*dharmas*) during severe calamities, and the maintenance of clear wisdom. It emphasizes an interconnected cosmic support system where gross elements, senses, and bodies mutually sustain one another across sub-human, human, and celestial realms.

---

## Vaishnava Relevance

In devotional Vaishnavism, particularly outlined in the *Sri Bhakti-rasamrta-sindhu* (Verse II.vi.145), *dhrti* describes the profound steadiness of the heart that emerges from direct realization of the Supreme Lord, freedom from existential suffering, and the deep cultivation of divine love (*prema*). In this elevated spiritual state, an individual experiences no lamentation for unfulfilled desires or lost worldly objects, and qualities like *ksama* (tolerance) naturally merge into this steady emotional framework (*dhrti-bhava*).
