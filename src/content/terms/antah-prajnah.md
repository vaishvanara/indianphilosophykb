Meaning: "inwardly conscious or aware of internal objects; an epithet of *Taijasa* in the dream state (*svapna*) in *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* (verse 4), negated in *Turīya* (verse 7)."

See: [Mandukya Upanishad (Verse 4)](mandukyopanisat.md)

---

## Definition

In ordinary and lexical Sanskrit usage, *antaḥ-prajñaḥ* (Sanskrit: अन्तःप्रज्ञः) is an adjectival compound literally meaning "inwardly conscious," "having consciousness directed inward," or "cognizant of internal or mental objects." It combines the indeclinable adverbial prefix *antar* / *antaḥ* ("within," "inside," "interior," "inwardly") with *prajñā* / *prajña* ("consciousness," "cognition," "awareness").

In Upanishadic literature and classical Vedantic philosophy, most prominently in verse 4 of the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*, *antaḥ-prajñaḥ* serves as a key technical term designating the cognitive mode of *Taijasa* (the "effulgent" Self), the second quarter (*pāda*) of the Self (*Ātman*). It characterizes awareness oriented toward internal or subtle mental impressions (*sūkṣma-viṣaya* / *vāsanā*) during the dream state (*svapna-sthāna*), distinguishing it from waking consciousness (*bahiṣ-prajñaḥ*, "outwardly conscious") and deep sleep (*prājña*, "unified mass of consciousness").

---

## Etymology

Etymologically, *antaḥ-prajñaḥ* is a Sanskrit possessive compound (*bahuvrīhi samāsa*) parsed as follows:

1. **First member:** *Antaḥ* / *antar* (अन्तः / अन्तर्), an indeclinable adverbial particle or preposition signifying "within," "inside," "interior," or "inwardly."
2. **Second member:** *Prajñā* (प्रज्ञा) or *prajña* (प्रज्ञ), a noun derived from the Sanskrit verbal root *jñā* (ज्ञा, "to know" or "to cognize") prefixed by *pra-* (प्र-, "forth," "forward"), meaning "cognition," "consciousness," or "awareness."
3. **Grammatical form:** As a masculine nominative singular adjective (*antaḥ-prajñaḥ*), it functions as an adjectival attribute agreeing with "the second quarter" (*dvitīyaḥ pādaḥ*) or "the effulgent dreamer" (*Taijasaḥ*).

## Antaḥ-Prajñaḥ in the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad

In the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* (verses 4 and 7) and its classical commentary tradition (*Gauḍapāda Kārikā* and Śaṅkarācārya's *Bhāṣya*), *antaḥ-prajñaḥ* plays a crucial role in mapping the states of Consciousness (*avasthātraya*).

### Textual locus (verse 4)

Verse 4 defines the second quarter of the Self: "The second quarter is *Taijasa*, whose sphere of activity is the dream state (*svapna-sthāna*), who is conscious of internal objects (*antaḥ-prajñaḥ*), who has seven limbs and nineteen mouths, and who is the experiencer of subtle objects (*sūkṣma-bhuk*)."

### Doctrinal meaning and cognitive architecture

1. **Internal orientation (*antar-viṣaya*):** In the dream state (*svapna*), external physical senses (*jñānendriya* and *karmendriya*) are quieted or withdrawn into the internal organ (*antaḥkaraṇa*). Consciousness (*caitanya*), conditioned by the mind and activated by latent impressions (*vāsanā* or *saṁskāra*) collected during previous waking experiences, projects an internal universe of subtle subjects and objects.
2. **Substratum and instruments:** *Taijasa* ("the effulgent one") experiences these subtle mental forms through nineteen internal instruments (the mind, intellect, ego, recollection, vital airs, and subtle sense-faculties acting internally without physical sense organs) and is therefore called the enjoyer of subtle objects (*sūkṣma-bhuk*).
3. **Contrast with other states:** While *bahiṣ-prajñaḥ* in the waking state relies on physical contact between outer sense organs and gross external matter (*sthūla-viṣaya*), *antaḥ-prajñaḥ* relies purely on internal mental projections (*manomaya-prapañca*). In deep sleep (*suṣupti*), both outward and inward cognitions subside into an undifferentiated mass of consciousness (*prajñānaghana*).
4. **Negation in Turīya (verse 7):** In verse 7, the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* defines *Turīya* (the "Fourth", Pure Consciousness) through systematic apophatic negation (*neti neti*), declaring that *Turīya* is "neither inwardly conscious (*na antaḥ-prajñam*) nor outwardly conscious (*na bahiṣ-prajñam*), nor conscious of both simultaneously (*na ubhayataḥ-prajñam*)." By negating *antaḥ-prajñaḥ*, the Upaniṣad reveals that the subtle subject-object duality of dreams is an illusory superimposition upon the non-dual Absolute (*Brahman* / *Ātman*).
