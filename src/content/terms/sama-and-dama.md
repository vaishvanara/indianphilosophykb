# Śama and Dama

## The Pillars of Mental Mastery and Sensory Restraint

In Classical Indian psychology and Advaita Vedānta, **Śama (शम)** and **Dama (दम)** represent the essential foundational disciplines for attaining mental clarity, inner tranquility, and Self-knowledge. They form the first two virtues of **Ṣaṭka-Sampatti** (or *Ṣaḍ-Sampat*), the "sixfold wealth" of inner treasures that also includes *Uparati* (cessation), *Titikṣā* (forbearance), *Śraddhā* (faith/dedication), and *Samādhāna* (concentration). 

Together, these six virtues constitute the third major pillar of **Sādhanā-Chatuṣṭaya**—the fourfold spiritual qualification articulated by Adi Shankaracharya in *Vivekachudamani* and his commentaries on the *Brahma Sutras* and *Upanishads*, following *Viveka* (discrimination between the eternal and ephemeral) and *Vairāgya* (dispassion), and preceding *Mumukṣutva* (intense longing for liberation).

---

### 1. Śama (शम): Mastery and Quietude of the Mind

The word **Śama** derives from the Sanskrit root *śam*, meaning "to quiet, calm, or pacify". In the classical texts, Śama refers specifically to the control, mastery, and quietude of the internal organ (*antaḥkaraṇa*), which comprises the mind (*manas*), intellect (*buddhi*), memory (*citta*), and ego (*ahaṁkāra*).

* **Scriptural Definitions**: 
  * In *Vivekachudamani* (verse 22), Adi Shankara defines Śama as the state in which the mind, having repeatedly observed the inherent defects and illusory nature of sensual objects (*viṣaya-vrāta*) through discrimination and dispassion, is withdrawn from external attractions and steadily anchored in its own goal (*sva-lakṣya*), the Ātman.
  * In *Aparokṣānubhūti*, Adi Shankara defines Śama as the constant abandonment of latent desire-promptings (*vāsanā-tyāga*).
  * In *Tattva Bodha*, Śama is characterized straightforwardly as mental control or the discipline of thought.
* **Core Function**: Śama does not mean the suppression or annihilation of thoughts, but rather the cultivation of inner governance and emotional stability. It is the capacity to observe mental impulses as they arise and consciously choose to redirect the mind away from unhelpful distractions toward Self-inquiry. As noted in the *Bhagavad Gita* (6.26), Śama involves bringing the restless mind back to the Self whenever and wherever it wanders.
* **Mind as the Commander**: The mind functions as the commander of the entire sensory apparatus. When the mind is calmed at its root by eradicating latent *vāsanās* (desires), serenity (*śānti*) arises naturally, making Śama the primary qualification for an aspirant seeking spiritual clarity.

---

### 2. Dama (दम): Intelligent Restraint of the Senses

Derived from the root *dam* (meaning "to restrain, control, or subdue"), **Dama** refers to the governance and discipline of the external sense organs (*bāhyendriya*). 

* **Scope of Discipline**: Dama covers all ten sensory channels (*indriyas*), categorized into:
  1. The five **Jñānendriyas** (organs of perception): eyes, ears, skin, tongue, and nose.
  2. The five **Karmendriyas** (organs of action): speech (*vāk*), hands (*pāṇi*), feet (*pāda*), excretory organs, and reproductive organs.
* **Scriptural Definitions**:
  * In *Vivekachudamani* (verse 23), Dama is defined as turning both types of organs away from objects of enjoyment (*viṣayas*) and restricting them to their respective spheres or proper orbits (*sva-sva-golake*).
  * In *Aparokṣānubhūti* (verse 6), Dama is defined as the restraint of the external functions of the organs.
* **Judicious Moderation vs. Suppression**: A central emphasis in Vedāntic literature is that Dama is **not** harsh bodily suppression, physical self-mutilation, or the blunting of the senses through misguided austerities. The body is viewed as a sacred instrument ("a moving temple") that must be kept healthy and functional. Dama is intelligent, judicious restraint—establishing a healthy boundary or orbit for sensory activities so that the senses serve as useful servants rather than wild masters.

### 3. The Interplay and Dynamics Between Śama and Dama

Although Śama addresses the internal mind and Dama addresses external behavior, they operate in close synergy:

1. **First and Second Lines of Defense**:
   * **Śama** serves as the **first line of defense** by evaluating and regulating desires at the mental stage before they manifest as outward behavior.
   * **Dama** serves as the **second line of defense** when impulses or emotions (such as anger or craving) surge suddenly before the mind can deliberate; Dama holds back physical action, preventing the senses from acting out and causing fresh karmic entanglements.
2. **The Chariot Analogy**: 
   * The *Katha Upanishad* presents a famous metaphor where the individual (*Jīva*) is the master in the chariot, the body is the chariot, the intellect (*buddhi*) is the charioteer, the mind (*manas*) is the reins, and the senses (*indriyas*) are wild horses. Śama represents keeping the reins firm, while Dama represents keeping the horses from running amok toward destructive temptations.
3. **Mutual Interdependence**:
   * Śama provides the inner groundwork for Dama because sense organs cannot operate independently without the mind's attention and cooperation.
   * Conversely, Dama protects Śama because when alluring sense objects appear, physically restraining the senses prevents the mind from being overwhelmed by fresh temptations.

### 4. Scriptural Foundations and Classical Commentaries

Śama and Dama are rooted in ancient Vedic and Vedāntic texts:

* **Brihadaranyaka Upanishad**:
  * In chapter IV.4.23, Sage Yajnavalkya instructs King Janaka that a true seeker who realizes the Ātman becomes *śānta* (endowed with Śama), *dānta* (endowed with Dama), *uparata* (detached), *titikṣu* (forbearing), and *samāhita* (concentrated).
  * In chapter V.2.1, Prajapati gives the single syllable **"Da"** to Devas, Humans, and Asuras; the Devas interpret it as *Dāmyata* ("Control your senses"). Kanchi Maha-Swamigal explains that "Devas" allegorically represents individuals with high intellectual or ethical gifts who nevertheless struggle with sensory temptation.
* **Brahma Sutras & Bhagavad Gita**:
  * *Brahma Sutra* (III.4.27) explicitly decrees that the seeker must be equipped with Śama, Dama, and the rest (*śama-damādy-upetaḥ syāt*) as ordained by the Vedas for attaining Self-knowledge.
  * The *Bhagavad Gita* repeatedly highlights both Śama and Dama as divine qualities (*daivī sampad*) and essential practices for meditation and self-mastery (Gita 3.7, 6.24, 6.26, 8.12, 16.1-2).
* **Mahanarayana Upanishad**:
  * This text notes that Dama is the supreme discipline for the *Brahmachari* (student phase), while Śama is the supreme discipline for the *Muni* or *Sannyasi* (renunciate), highlighting how sensory restraint lays the groundwork for complete mental quietude in mature reflection.

---

### 5. Psychological Homeostasis and Modern Relevance

In modern psychological terms, the practice of Śama and Dama addresses what Swami Ranganathananda described as **inner stability** or **psychic homeostasis**. 

* **Countering Impulse-Driven Culture**: Modern commercial environments frequently exploit the absence of Śama and Dama, encouraging compulsive consumption, instant gratification, and reactive behaviors under consumerist slogans.
* **Cultivating Freedom and Purpose**: Without Śama and Dama, individuals remain perpetual captives to fleeting sensory impulses and environmental triggers. Practicing intelligent self-restraint transforms the senses from chaotic masters into aligned instruments, fostering mental poise, physical health, meaningful relationships, and authentic inner freedom.
