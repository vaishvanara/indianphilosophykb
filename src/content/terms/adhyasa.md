Meaning: "the false superimposition or cognitive illusion of attributing the qualities of one object to another, such as non-Self to Self."

---

## Definition

Within Advaita Vedānta, *adhyāsa* (Sanskrit: अध्यास; IAST: *adhyāsa*) is defined as false cognitive superimposition, erroneous attribution, or illusion. 

In his foundational commentary on the *Brahmasūtra* (*Adhyāsa-bhāṣya*), Śaṅkarācārya defines *adhyāsa* as "the appearance, in the form of a memory, of something previously experienced in some other place" (*smṛti-rūpaḥ paratra pūrvadṛṣṭāvabhāsaḥ*). It is succinctly characterized as "the cognition of a thing as something else where it is not" (*atasmin tad-buddhiḥ*) or as attributing the properties of an unreal entity (*avastu*) onto a real entity (*vastu*). 

Epistemologically, *adhyāsa* is the fundamental error wherein the attributes of the insentient, objective world (*anātman*—including body, mind, and senses) are falsely projected onto the self-luminous, subjective Consciousness (*Ātman* / *Brahman*), and vice versa.

---

## Etymology

Morphologically, *adhyāsa* is a masculine Sanskrit noun formed from the prefix *adhi-* (अधि-, "over," "upon," or "above") combined with the verbal root *as* (अस्, "to cast," "to throw," or "to place"), followed by the nominalizing suffix *ghañ*. 

Grammatically and etymologically, it denotes "a casting over," "placing upon," or "superimposition". Linguistically, Advaitic exegesis distinguishes *adhyāsa* from simple memory (*smṛti*) or ordinary cognitive error. 

While memory reproduces past experiences of an absent object, *adhyāsa* involves the immediate, present projection of past mental impressions (*saṃskāra* or *vāsanā*) onto a currently perceived substratum (*adhiṣṭhāna*).

---

## Adhyāsa in Advaita Vedānta

In the doctrinal architecture of Advaita Vedānta, *adhyāsa* is the central epistemological mechanism accounting for human bondage (*saṃsāra*), individual soulhood (*jīvatva*), and the experience of phenomenal duality (*prapañca*). The fundamental problem of existence stems from subject-object superimposition (*viṣayi-viṣaya-adhyāsa*): although the subjective Self (*viṣayī*, Pure Consciousness) and the objective non-Self (*viṣaya*, matter and mind) are as opposed in nature as light and darkness, human experience begins with their natural (*naisargika*) and beginningless (*anādi*) mutual superimposition (*itaretara-adhyāsa*).

Advaita Vedānta classifies *adhyāsa* into several technical taxonomy categories:

1. **Svarūpa-adhyāsa and saṁsarga-adhyāsa**: *Svarūpa-adhyāsa* is the superimposition of an illusory object itself onto a real locus (e.g., perceiving a false snake on a real rope). *Saṁsarga-adhyāsa* is the superimposition of a false relation or attribute onto an object (e.g., perceiving a clear crystal as red due to proximity to a red flower).
2. **Artha-adhyāsa and jñāna-adhyāsa**: *Artha-adhyāsa* denotes the objective superimposition itself (the illusory object or world appearance), whereas *jñāna-adhyāsa* denotes the subjective cognitive illusion or false knowledge regarding it.
3. **Dharma-adhyāsa and dharmi-adhyāsa**: *Dharma-adhyāsa* is the attribution of properties or qualities (such as agency, enjoyership, old age, or gender) to the Self, whereas *dharmi-adhyāsa* is the fundamental misidentification of the entity itself (identifying the Self with the physical body).

Doctrinally, *adhyāsa* serves as the indispensable prerequisite (*adhikāra*) for all empirical cognition (*pramāṇa*), knowership (*pramātṛtva*), and worldly as well as ritualistic activities (*vyavahāra*). Only when pure Consciousness is falsely identified with the body-mind complex does the ego-sense arise, giving rise to the notions of "I" and "mine" (*ahaṃ-mamātā*). When right discriminative knowledge (*vidyā* or *samyag-jñāna*) dawns, *adhyāsa* is sublated (*bādha*), exposing the world-appearance as non-real (*mithyā*) and revealing the absolute non-duality of *Ātman* and *Brahman*.

## Primary texts and commentaries

- **Śaṅkarācārya’s *Adhyāsa-bhāṣya* (*Brahmasūtra-bhāṣya* Introduction)**: Serves as the foundational treatise on superimposition. Śaṅkara establishes that all human experience rests upon mutual superimposition (*itaretara-adhyāsa*) of the Self and non-Self, defining *adhyāsa* and demonstrating that it is beginningless (*anādi*), natural (*naisargika*), and the cause of all agency and enjoyership.
- **Padmapāda’s *Pañcapādikā* (first *varṇaka*)**: Provides an extensive, detailed commentary expanding Śaṅkara’s definition of *adhyāsa*. Padmapāda defines superimposition as "the appearance of the form of something in something that does not really have that form" (*atadrūpe tadrūpāvabhāsaḥ*), which is false (*mithyā*). He demonstrates that *adhyāsa* requires mental impressions (*saṃskāras*) generated from past experience combined with a vitiated cognitive cause.
- **Śaṅkarācārya’s *Upadeśasāhasrī* (prose Ch. 1–2; Verse Ch. 18)**: Analyzes how the disciple falsely attributes physical and mental attributes to the actionless Self, demonstrating that actions and modifications belong to the body-mind complex rather than the witness-consciousness (*sākṣin*).
- **Vidyāraṇya’s *Pañcadaśī* (1.24–25; 1.33–35; 6.224–226)**: Details the mutual superimposition between the immutable Self (*Kūṭastha*) and the reflected consciousness (*jīva*/*cidābhāsa*), illustrating how the existence and selfhood of *Ātman* are wrongly transferred to the ego-entity, like silver superimposed on mother-of-pearl.
- ***Vivekacūḍāmaṇi* (vv. 179, 196–198, 235–236, 277, 281–282, 407)**: Attributes the bondage of transmigration (*saṃsṛti*) to the defect of superimposition (*adhyāsa-doṣa*) created by the mind. It repeatedly employs the rope-snake paradigm (*rajju-sarpa-nyāya*) to show that the superimposed world has no existence apart from its substratum (*adhiṣṭhāna*), and instructs the seeker to diligently eliminate superimposition (*svādhyāsāpanaya*) through Self-realization.
- ***Rāma Gītā* (text XXXVII)**: Synthesizes the definition of *adhyāsa* as perceiving a thing to be something other than itself due to delusion (*yad anyad anyatra vibhāvyate bhramād adhyāsam*), giving the example of seeing a snake in a rope.

## Interpretations and disputes

1. **The locus objection (*adhiṣṭhāna* of Adhyāsa)**: In the *Adhyāsa-bhāṣya*, an objector contends that superimposition is impossible on the inner Self (*pratyag-ātman*), because superimposition normally requires a frontally presented object (like a rope or shell), whereas the Self is non-objective and unattached. Śaṅkara refutes this by arguing that: (a) the Self is not entirely a non-object, as it is immediately evident in everyone's "I"-notion (*ahaṁ-pratyaya*); and (b) superimposition does not strictly require sensory objecthood, as people superimpose blueness and a dome-like shape onto the sky (*ākāśa*), which is not a visible physical object.
2. **Adhyāsa vs. avidyā as cause and effect**: Modern and classical scholars debate whether *adhyāsa* and *avidyā* are identical or distinct. In Śaṅkara’s authentic texts, *avidyā* is frequently identified directly with superimposition (*adhyāsa*). However, post-Śaṅkara commentators (such as Maṇḍana Miśra, Padmapāda, and Vācaspati Miśra) developed a distinction between causal or root ignorance (*mūlāvidyā* / *ācchādika-avidyā*) and resultant or secondary superimposition (*tulāvidyā* / *vikṣepikā-avidyā*). Modern scholars like Swami Sacchidanandendra Sarasvati critique this distinction, contending that Śaṅkara viewed *avidyā* strictly as cognitive superimposition (*adhyāsa*) rather than an ontological material cause.
3. **Rival theories of error (*Khyātivāda*)**: Advaita's theory of superimposition (*anirvacanīya-khyāti*) disputes alternative Indian theories of cognitive error:
   - ***Anyathā-khyāti* (Nyāya)**: Claims error is the misapprehension of a real object existing elsewhere projected onto present space. Advaita refutes this by arguing that an object located elsewhere cannot be immediately perceived here.
   - ***Akhyāti* (Prābhākara Mīmāṃsā / Sāṅkhya)**: Claims error is mere non-apprehension (*agrahaṇa*) or non-discrimination between a present perception and a remembered object. Advaita refutes this by showing that non-apprehension cannot account for positive action (such as reaching for silver or fleeing from a snake).
   - ***Anirvacanīya-khyāti* (Advaita Vedānta)**: Asserts that the superimposed snake or world appearance is neither absolutely real (*sat*), nor absolutely unreal (*asat*), nor both, but logically indefinable (*anirvacanīya*) during the illusion, vanishing upon sublation (*bādha*) by true knowledge.
