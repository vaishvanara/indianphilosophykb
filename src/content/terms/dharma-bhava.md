# Dharma Bhāva - धर्मभाव

In Indian philosophical and spiritual traditions, profound transformations begin not merely in external actions, but in the internal state of consciousness. One of the central concepts capturing this inner orientation is **Dharma Bhāva** (धर्मभाव)—a Sanskrit compound that describes the inward feeling, mental disposition, and attitude aligned with righteousness, cosmic order, and spiritual truth.

---

### 1. Etymology and Linguistic Roots

The term *Dharma Bhāva* is composed of two primary Sanskrit words:

* **Dharma (धर्म):** Derived from the verbal root **धृ (*dhṛ*)**, which means "to hold," "to support," "to sustain," or "to uphold." *Dharma* signifies that which sustains the individual, society, and the cosmos. Depending on the context, it translates to righteousness, virtue, universal moral law, inherent nature, or ethical duty.
* **Bhāva (भाव):** Derived from the verbal root **भू (*bhū*)**, which means "to be" or "to become." *Bhāva* refers to a state of being, mental disposition, inner feeling, attitude, or sentiment.

Combined, **Dharma Bhāva** literally translates to the **"state of being established in Dharma"** or the **"inner attitude of righteousness."** It is not simply the outward observance of rules or rituals; rather, it is the subjective, deeply rooted inclination of the heart and mind toward virtue, balance, and truth.

---

### 2. Origins and Classical Foundations

The concept of *bhāva* as an internal psychological and spiritual state appears prominently across classical Indian systems:

* **Sāṅkhya and Classical Yoga:** In the *Sāṅkhya Kārikā* (verse 23), the intellect (*buddhi*) possesses inherent dispositions (*bhāvas*). The four luminous, *sāttvika* dispositions are **Dharma** (virtue/righteousness), **Jñāna** (knowledge), **Vairāgya** (dispassion), and **Aiśvarya** (spiritual mastery/power). Here, *Dharma Bhāva* is recognized as a fundamental quality of an elevated, purified intellect that naturally gravitates away from base instincts and toward higher order.
* **The Epics and Purāṇas:** In texts like the *Mahābhārata* and *Bhagavad Gītā*, characters are assessed not only by the consequences of their deeds, but by their *bhāva*—the spirit and intention behind their actions. A deed done without *dharma-bhāva* (devoid of noble intent) lacks spiritual merit.

---

### 3. Meaning and Use in Vedānta

In Advaita and other schools of **Vedānta**, *Dharma Bhāva* plays a foundational role in the spiritual seeker’s progression toward *Moksha* (liberation):

#### A. The Foundation for *Citta-Śuddhi* (Purification of the Mind)
Vedānta maintains that direct knowledge of the Self (*Brahmajñāna*) is only possible in a mind free of agitation (*vikṣepa*) and impurity (*mala*). Cultivating *Dharma Bhāva* refines the intellect (*buddhi*), replacing selfish desires (*kāma*) and aversion (*dveṣa*) with harmony, compassion, and moral clarity. Without this ethical foundation, the highest non-dual teachings remain mere intellectual concepts rather than direct experience.

#### B. The Spirit of *Karma Yoga*
In the Vedantic interpretation of the *Bhagavad Gītā*, *Karma Yoga* requires action performed with *Īśvara-arpaṇa-buddhi* (dedication to the Divine) and *prasāda-buddhi* (acceptance of results as grace). This selfless approach is the practical manifestation of *Dharma Bhāva*. When an aspirant acts from *Dharma Bhāva*, work ceases to generate binding *karmic* impressions and instead becomes an instrument of spiritual purification.

#### C. Alignment with the Cosmic Order (*Ṛta* / *Īśvara*)
In Advaita Vedānta, the empirical universe is sustained by *Īśvara* through the principle of *Dharma*. An individual who possesses *Dharma Bhāva* lives in attunement with this cosmic order. Their personal will aligns with the universal will, creating an inner stillness that makes the seeker receptive to the inquiry into the Absolute (*ātma-vicāra*).

#### D. The Natural Expression of the *Jīvanmukta*
While an aspiring seeker (*sādhaka*) consciously cultivates *Dharma Bhāva* through discipline, a realized sage (*jīvanmukta*) embodies it spontaneously. Having realized the unity of all existence in *Brahman*, the liberated soul cannot act unrighteously; their natural state of being is synonymous with *Dharma Bhāva*, radiating universal goodwill (*sarvabhūta-hite ratāḥ*).

In essence, **Dharma Bhāva** is the bridge between ethics and metaphysics in Indian thought—it transforms duty from an external obligation into an inward state of grace and wisdom.
