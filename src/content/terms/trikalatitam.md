Meaning: "the threefold division of time; that which transcends the three divisions of time (past, present, and future), designating the timeless Absolute (*Brahman*).

See: [Mandukya Upanishad (Verse 1)](mandukyopanisat.md)

## Definition

The compound *trikālātītaṃ* (Sanskrit: त्रिकालातीतं) combines two principal semantic components: *trikāla* (the triad of time, encompassing past, present, and future) and *atīta* (gone beyond, transcended). Literally, the expression signifies "that which has passed beyond or crossed over the three temporal periods."

In its idiomatic and context-dependent usage within Vedantic exegesis, the term does not merely denote something ancient or long-lasting; rather, it identifies a mode of Being or Consciousness that is entirely unconditioned by temporal succession (*kālāparicchinna*). While the empirical cosmos and its cognitive states operate strictly within temporal boundaries, *trikālātītaṃ* specifies the transcendent Reality, identified with *Turīya* or unconditioned *Brahman*, that serves as the timeless substratum of all temporal experience.

---

## Etymology

The Sanskrit compound noun/adjective *trikālātītaṃ* is grammatically analyzed through its two constituent members: *trikāla* and *atīta*. 

The first member, *trikāla*, combines the numeral prefix *tri-* ("three") with the nominal stem *kāla* ("time" or "duration") to form a numerical compound classified as a *dvigu-samāsa* (specifically a *samāhāra-dvigu*, expressing an aggregate); its *vigraha-vākya* (compound expansion) is *trayāṇāṁ kālānām samāhāraḥ*, meaning "the aggregate of the three times: past, present, and future." 

The second member, *atīta*, derives from the verbal prefix *ati* ("beyond" or "past") prefixed to the verbal root *i* ("to go") along with the primary *kṛt* suffix *-kta* (signifying completed action), where morphological internal *savarṇa-dīrgha-sandhi* merges the short *i* vowels (*ati* + *ita* / *īta*) into the long *ī*. 

When combined, the compound is categorized as a *dvitīyā-tatpuruṣa* (an accusative determinative compound) in which the second member governs the first in the accusative case, represented by the primary *vigraha-vākya* *trikālam atītam iti trikālātītam* ("that which has gone beyond / transcended the three times"). 

Phonetically, the junction undergoes vocalic *savarṇa-dīrgha-sandhi*, merging the stem-final vowel *-a* of *trikāla* with the initial *-a-* of *atīta* into the long vowel *-ā-* (*trikālātīta*). In its complete inflectional form, the term takes the nominative/accusative singular neuter ending with the *anusvāra*/*m-kāra* (*-ṃ* / *-m*), thereby agreeing syntactically with the neuter relative pronoun phrase *yac cānyat* ("and whatever else") in the opening verse of the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*.

## Trikālātītaṃ in the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad

The expression *trikālātītaṃ* occurs in the very first verse of the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*:

> ओमित्येतदक्षरमिदं सर्वं तस्योपव्याख्यानं भूतं भवद्भविष्यदिति सर्वमोङ्कार एव । यच्चान्‍यत् त्रिकालातीतं तदप्योङ्कार एव ॥ १ ॥
> 
> *oṃ ity etad akṣaram idaṃ sarvaṃ tasyopavyākhyānaṃ bhūtaṃ bhavad bhaviṣyad iti sarvam oṅkāra eva | yac cānyat trikālātītaṃ tad apy oṅkāra eva || 1 ||*

### Grammatical function and textual context

Grammatically, *trikālātītaṃ* functions as a neuter adjectival predicate in apposition to *yac cānyat* ("whatever else there is"). The verse establishes a comprehensive exposition (*upavyākhyāna*) of the sacred syllable *Oṅkāra* (AUM) as coextensive with all existence (*idaṃ sarvam*). 

The text first defines *sarvam* ("everything") across the three empirical dimensions of time: *bhūtaṃ* (the past), *bhavad* (the present), and *bhaviṣyad* (the future), declaring that all temporal manifestations are nothing other than *Oṃkāra* (*sarvam oṅkāra eva*). Immediately following this temporal taxonomy, the *Upaniṣad* introduces the clause *yac cānyat trikālātītaṃ tad apy oṅkāra eva*, which asserts that whatever reality exists beyond or transcendent of the threefold division of time is likewise verily *Oṃkāra*.

### Philosophical significance

The inclusion of *trikālātītaṃ* in verse 1 resolves a central metaphysical question in Vedantic ontology: whether sacred sound (*Oṃkāra*) is merely a finite symbol bound to phenomenal creation or the ultimate Reality itself. By framing *Oṃkāra* as both the totality of empirical time and that which transcends time (*trikālātītaṃ*), the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* bridges the immanent cosmos with the transcendent Absolute.

### Exegetical layers: Upaniṣad, Śaṅkara, and Gauḍapāda

When interpreting this passage, the Advaita Vedānta tradition distinguishes three complementary layers of text and commentary. First, the primary *Upaniṣad* text focuses directly on the sacred utterance (*oṃkāra*) as the cosmic matrix and supreme reality, bridging temporal existence with timeless transcendence. 

Building upon this, Śaṅkarācārya's commentary (*Māṇḍūkyopaniṣadbhāṣya*) explains that *trikālātītaṃ* refers to both the unmanifested causal state (*avyākṛta*) and the unconditioned Fourth state (*Turīya* / *Brahman*). Śaṅkara argues that time (*kāla*) is itself an effect (*kārya*) of cosmic manifestation, meaning that the ultimate cause or non-dual substratum of existence cannot be delimited by time (*kālāparicchinna*). 

Finally, Gauḍapāda's *Kārikā* (*Āgama Prakaraṇa*) elaborates the *Upaniṣad*'s fourfold schema by mapping the temporal states of waking (*viśva*), dream (*taijasa*), and deep sleep (*prājña*) to the phonetic *mātrās* A, U, and M. Gauḍapāda identifies the unconditioned, timeless *Turīya* with the soundless *amātra* aspect of AUM, thereby reinforcing the temporal transcendence designated by *trikālātītaṃ*.

## Interpretations and disputes

### Translation nuances

Scholars and translators exhibit subtle differences in rendering *trikālātītaṃ*. Swami Nikhilananda translates the clause as "whatever else there is, beyond the threefold division of time." Other academic renders emphasize the verbal action of the participle, translating it as "that which has transcended the three times" or "the timeless Reality."

### Causal unmanifest (avyākṛta) vs. absolute transcendent (Turīya)

A key exegetical debate within Advaita circles concerns whether *trikālātītaṃ* in verse 1 refers primarily to the unmanifested causal principle (*avyākṛta* / *kāraṇātman*) or directly to the ultimate Fourth (*Turīya*).

Some commentators note that *avyākṛta* (the unmanifest state preceding cosmic projection) is *trikālātīta* in the sense of being unconditioned by empirical past, present, or future, yet it still contains the seed of time (*kāla-bīja*).

Śaṅkara clarifies that while the unmanifest causal state may be described as beyond empirical time, true liberation (*mokṣa*) pertains only to *Turīya*, which is entirely free from both cause (*kāraṇa*) and effect (*kārya*).

### Ontological status of Oṃkāra (Nāma-Nāmī-Abheda)

A major philosophical dispute exists between Advaita Vedānta and dualistic traditions regarding the semantic relationship between the name (*nāma*) and the named object (*nāmī*)

Advaita Vedānta (Śaṅkara & Gauḍapāda) asserts the absolute identity (*abheda*) of *Oṃkāra* and *Brahman*. *Oṃkāra* is not a mere arbitrary indicator; the sound-substratum and the ontological substratum are identical.

Non-Advaitic commentators, as noted in comparative treatises (e.g., Potter's *Encyclopedia*), treat *Oṃkāra* primarily as a symbolic emblem (*pratīka*) or reverential designation of the personal Godhead (*Īśvara*), rejecting complete ontological identity between the linguistic symbol and the transcendent Absolute.

### Textual status of the Māṇḍūkya text and Kārikā

As documented in historical surveys of Indian philosophy, there has been scholarly debate concerning the textual boundary of the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*. Standard Advaita tradition treats the 12 prose passages as the original *śruti* and Gauḍapāda's 215 verses as an expository commentary (*kārikā*).

Alternative traditions (including commentators within the Dvaita and Viśiṣṭādvaita schools) treated the first 29 *kārikās* of Book One (*Āgama Prakaraṇa*) as part of the revealed *śruti* text itself, leading to distinct structural boundaries in their textual analysis of Mantra 1 and its immediate context.
