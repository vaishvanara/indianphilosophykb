Meaning: "the Absolute;" the ultimate, non-dual, infinite Reality, pure consciousness, and absolute ground of all existence in Advaita Vedānta

See: [Mandukya Upanishad (Verse 2)](mandukyopanisat.md)

---

## Definition

In Sanskrit lexical and classical usage, *brahman* (Sanskrit: ब्रह्मन्) literally signifies that which is great, expansive, or immense, derived from the verbal root *bṛh* ("to grow, expand, or become great"). In the earliest Vedic contexts, the term carries the ordinary meaning of sacred prayer, holy utterance, devotional text, or the sacred wisdom of the Veda itself. 

In its extended and technical philosophical usage within Advaita Vedānta, *Brahman* denotes the unconditioned Supreme Reality (*paramārthika-satya*) or the non-dual (*advaita*), self-luminous (*svayaṁprakāśa*) substratum (*adhiṣṭhāna*) upon which the entire phenomenal universe is superimposed. Scriptural traditions define *Brahman* through its essential nature (*svarūpa-lakṣaṇa*) as pure Existence, Consciousness, and Infinity or Bliss (*satyam jñānam anantam brahma* / *saccidānanda*). 

Relational definitions (*taṭastha-lakṣaṇa*) describe *Brahman* as the ultimate cause of the origination, sustentation, and reabsorption of the universe (*janmādyasya yataḥ*). Advaita distinguishes between *Nirguṇa Brahman* (the attributeless, partless Absolute beyond speech and conceptualization) and *Saguṇa Brahman* (also termed *Īśvara*), which represents the Absolute as conditioned by the creative power of *māyā* to function as the personal Lord and ruler of the cosmos.

## Etymology

*Brahman* is derived from the Sanskrit root *bṛh* (or *bṛṃh*), which signifies "to grow", "to expand", "to swell", or "to increase". Grammatically, it is formed as a neuter noun (*brahman*, nominative singular *brahma*) taking the primary nominal suffix *-man*. Etymologically, the term indicates "that which possesses unlimited greatness or the inherent capacity for infinite expansion" (*bṛhattvāt bṛṃhaṇatvāccau*).

Grammarians and commentators carefully distinguish the neuter noun *Brahman* (Absolute Reality, sacred word) from related Sanskrit forms:

- The masculine noun *brahmān* (nominative singular *brahmā*), which denotes either the personal creator deity in the Hindu pantheon or the chief officiating priest in Vedic rituals who supervises the sacrifice.
- The derivative noun *brāhmaṇa*, which refers to a member of the priestly class or to the prose Vedic commentaries on ritual acts.

## Brahman in Advaita Vedānta

In Advaita Vedānta, *Brahman* serves as the central metaphysical principle, denoting the sole, indivisible Reality. The *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* encapsulates this doctrine through the formula *AUM ity etad akṣaram idam sarvam* ("AUM, the word, is all this") and the *mahāvākya* *ayam ātmā brahma* ("This Self is Brahman"), establishing the absolute identity of the individual Self (*Ātman*) and the Supreme Reality (*Brahman*).

The *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* systematically maps *Brahman* / *Ātman* across four quarters (*pādas*), correlated with the phonetic components of the sacred syllable *AUM*:

1. **First quarter (*Vaiśvānara* / *Viśva*)**: The waking state of consciousness, associated with the letter *A*, which cognizes external gross objects.
2. **Second quarter (*Taijasa*)**: The dream state of consciousness, associated with the letter *U*, which cognizes internal subtle objects projected by the mind.
3. **Third quarter (*Prājña*)**: The deep sleep state, associated with the letter *M*, characterized as an undifferentiated mass of consciousness and bliss (*prajñānaghana*), where desires and mental duality temporarily cease.
4. **Fourth quarter (*Turīya*)**: The partless, soundless (*amātra*) state beyond time and causation. *Turīya* is not a temporal fourth state alongside the other three, but the unconditioned, non-dual Absolute itself which is described as the cessation of all phenomena (*prapañcopaśama*), peaceful (*śānta*), auspicious (*śiva*), and non-dual (*advaita*).

In his foundational commentary (*kārikā*), Gauḍapāda formulates the doctrine of non-origination (*Ajātivāda*), demonstrating that *Brahman* never undergoes actual creation, birth, or modification. According to Gauḍapāda and Śaṅkarācārya, the apparent manifestation of the variegated universe is merely an illusory appearance (*vivarta*) brought about by *māyā* or *avidyā* (nescience), analogous to a rope mistakenly perceived as a snake in dim light. 

In Advaita doctrine, the individual transmigrating soul (*jīva*) is essentially non-different from *Brahman* (*brahmaiva jīvaḥ nāparaḥ*). The illusion of separation arises from limiting adjuncts (*upādhi*) superimposed upon pure consciousness. Liberation (*mokṣa*) is not the production of a new state, but the direct intuitive realization (*aparokṣānubhūti*) of one's eternal identity with *Brahman*, brought about through scriptural inquiry (*vicāra*) and the awakening to Vedic declarations such as *Tat Tvam Asi* ("That Thou Art") and *Aham Brahmāsmi* ("I am Brahman").

## Primary Texts and Commentaries

Primary passages establishing the nature of *Brahman* appear throughout the *Prasthānatrayī* (the Upaniṣad, *Brahmasūtra*, and *Bhagavad Gītā*):

- **Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad (Verses 1–12)**: Provides the primary textual exposition equating *Brahman*, *Ātman*, the quarters of consciousness, and the syllable *AUM*.
- **Taittirīya Upaniṣad (2.1.1)**: Defines the essential nature of *Brahman* as *satyam jñānam anantam brahma* ("Brahman is Truth, Knowledge, and Infinity").
- **Chāndogya Upaniṣad (6.2.1, 6.8.7)**: Declares *ekam evādvitīyam* ("One only, without a second") and reveals the non-difference of the Self in the instruction *tat tvam asi*.
- **Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad (1.4.10, 2.3.6, 3.8.8)**: Contains the declaration *aham brahmāsmi* ("I am Brahman") and defines *Brahman* via the apophatic method of *neti, neti* ("not this, not this").
- **Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad (1.1.6, 2.2.11)**: Describes *Brahman* as imperishable (*akṣara*), invisible, without attributes, and all-pervading.

Key commentaries and secondary treatises further elaborate this doctrine:

- **Gauḍapāda's Māṇḍūkya-kārikā**: Systematizes non-dual metaphysics across four chapters (*Āgama*, *Vaitathya*, *Advaita*, and *Alātaśānti*).
- **Śaṅkarācārya's commentaries**: His *Māṇḍūkyopaniṣad-bhāṣya*, *Gauḍapādakārikā-bhāṣya*, and *Brahmasūtrabhāṣya* establish non-duality through rigorous textual exegesis, establishing that *Brahman* is the uncaused material and efficient cause of the world from the relative standpoint and attributeless Absolute from the transcendental standpoint.
- **Post-Śaṅkara exegesis**: Works such as Sureśvara's *Naiṣkarmyasiddhi*, Śrī Sadāśiva Brahmendra's *Dṛg-Dṛśya-Viveka*, Vidyāraṇya's *Pañcadaśī*, and Sadānanda's *Vedāntasāra* preserve and refine Advaita methodology.
- **Modern scholarship**: Modern scholars such as Karl H. Potter (*Encyclopedia of Indian Philosophies*, Vol. 3) and Swami Nikhilananda provide historical, textual, and comparative contextualization of these classical commentaries.

## Interpretations and Disputes

Significant philosophical disputes exist both between Vedantic schools and within the Advaita tradition regarding the interpretation of *Brahman*:

### Advaita vs. Viśiṣṭādvaita and Dvaita

   - Śaṅkarācārya's Advaita Vedānta maintains absolute monism (*kevaladvaita*): *Brahman* is ultimately attributeless (*nirguṇa*), and all cosmic multiplicity is an ungrounded, apparent transformation (*vivarta*) caused by *māyā*.
   - Rāmānuja's Viśiṣṭādvaita (*Śrībhāṣya*) rejects the concept of an attributeless Absolute and the doctrine of an undefinable illusion (*anirvacanīya-māyā*). Rāmānuja argues that *Brahman* is eternally a personal God (*Puruṣottama*) endowed with infinite auspicious qualities (*saguṇa*), and that individual souls and material nature constitute the real, structural body (*śarīra*) of *Brahman*.
   - Madhva's Dvaita Vedānta rejects non-duality entirely, positing an absolute, unbridgeable difference (*bheda*) between *Brahman*, individual souls, and material objects.

### Intra-Advaita Disputes

   - **Refutation of Bhedābheda**: Śaṅkarācārya and Sureśvara explicitly refute the earlier Vedantic interpretation of Bhartṛprapañca, who proposed an identity-in-difference (*bhedābheda*) model wherein *Brahman* is simultaneously one and many, analogous to an ocean and its waves. Śaṅkara demonstrates that a partless, immutable entity cannot possess dual contradictory natures.
   - **Status of the Māṇḍūkya text**: Modern scholarship and traditional commentators note historical debates concerning whether Gauḍapāda's first twenty-nine verses (*Āgama Prakaraṇa*) were originally a commentary on the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* prose text or were regarded as part of the revealed *śruti* text itself.
