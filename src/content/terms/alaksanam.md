Meaning: "indefinable, uncharacterized, or devoid of empirical marks; an apophatic epithet of *Turīya* (Pure Consciousness) in the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*."

See: [Mandukya Upanishad (Verse 7)](mandukyopanisat.md)

---

## Definition

In lexical and ordinary Sanskrit usage, *alakṣaṇa* (neuter form: *alakṣaṇam*, Devanāgarī: अलक्षणम्) means "indefinable," "without any sign or mark," "devoid of characteristic features," or "unmarked." The primary term *lakṣaṇa* denotes a distinguishing feature, symptom, definition, or empirical mark by which an entity is identified or differentiated from others; hence, *alakṣaṇa* denotes the absence of any such attribute or defining characteristic.

In its extended philosophical sense within Upanishadic literature, most prominently in verse seven of the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*, *alakṣaṇam* functions as a technical apophatic epithet for the transcendent Reality, *Turīya* (the "Fourth" state of Pure Consciousness or the non-dual Self). Here, it indicates that Ultimate Reality transcends all empirical categories, possesses no perceptual or inferential marks (*liṅga* / *lakṣaṇa*), and cannot be defined, delimited, or conceptualized by the rational mind.

---

## Etymology

Etymologically, *alakṣaṇam* is a Sanskrit compound noun or adjective formed from two principal components:

1. The negative prefix *a-* (अ-), denoting negation, privation, or absence ("not," "without," "devoid of").
2. The neuter noun *lakṣaṇa* (लक्षण), derived from the Sanskrit verbal root *lakṣ* (लक्ष्), meaning "to mark," "to perceive," "to observe," or "to characterize." A *lakṣaṇa* is an essential characteristic, defining attribute, or inferential mark.

Grammatically, *alakṣaṇam* is the nominative and accusative singular neuter form of the adjective *alakṣaṇa* (masculine: *alakṣaṇaḥ*, feminine: *alakṣaṇā*). In scriptural usage, such as in the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*, the neuter gender matches the neuter subject *Caturtham* ("the Fourth quarter") or *Brahman* / *Ātman*.

## Alakṣaṇam in Advaita Vedānta

In Advaita Vedānta, particularly within the tradition of the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*, Gauḍapāda's *Kārikā*, and Śaṅkarācārya's commentary (*Bhāṣya*), *alakṣaṇam* plays a pivotal role in establishing the apophatic method (*via negativa* or *neti neti*) for revealing the nature of *Turīya* (Pure Consciousness).

### Exegesis of Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad (verse 7)

Verse 7 defines *Turīya* by systematically negating the attributes of the three relative states of experience: waking (*jāgrat* / *Viśva*), dreaming (*svapna* / *Taijasa*), and deep sleep (*suṣupti* / *Prājña*). To demonstrate that *Turīya* is completely unconditioned, the Upaniṣad provides six negative epithets specifying its transcendence over all instruments of human cognition:

- ***Adṛṣṭam* (unperceived):** Beyond the reach of the cognitive sense organs (*jñānendriya*).
- ***Avyavahāryam* (unrelatable):** Beyond all empirical transactions, linguistic conventions, or practical dealings (*vyavahāra*).
- ***Agrāhyam* (ungraspable):** Beyond the reach of the motor organs or organs of action (*karmendriya*).
- ***Alakṣaṇam* (indefinable / uncharacterized):** Devoid of any inferential mark (*liṅga*) or defining characteristic (*lakṣaṇa*) through which its existence could be deduced or classified.
- ***Acintyam* (unthinkable):** Beyond the realm of mental deliberation, imagination, or conceptual thought (*manas*).
- ***Avyapadeśyam* (inexpressible):** Beyond verbal designation, naming, or spoken description (*vāc*).

### Epistemological significance (*liṅga-rāhitya*)

In classical Indian epistemology (*pramāṇa-śāstra*), an unperceived object is typically known through inference (*anumāna*), which requires an inferential mark (*hetu* or *liṅga*), such as inferring fire from the presence of smoke. 

By describing *Turīya* as *alakṣaṇam*, Advaita Vedānta emphasizes that Pure Consciousness possesses no characteristics, qualities (*guṇa*), or logical marks (*liṅga*) that could serve as a middle term in an inferential syllogism. Consequently, Ultimate Reality cannot be arrived at through speculative logic or inferential reasoning.

### Semantic and hermeneutic role

Because *Turīya* is *alakṣaṇa* (devoid of features), words (*śabda*) cannot signify it through their primary, direct meaning (*vācyārtha*), which requires a genus (*jāti*), quality (*guṇa*), action (*kriyā*), or relation (*sambandha*). 

Advaita Vedānta establishes that scriptural Great Sayings (*mahāvākyas*) reveal *Brahman* only indirectly through secondary implication (*lakṣaṇā-vṛtti*, specifically *jahad-ajahal-lakṣaṇā*), negating false superimposed adjuncts (*upādhi*) while pointing to the self-luminous substratum.

### Positive grounding (*ekātma-pratyaya-sāra*)

Advaita commentary cautions that *alakṣaṇam* and related negations do not imply that *Turīya* is a void (*śūnya*) or non-entity. Following the negative qualifications, verse seven affirms *Turīya* positively as *ekātma-pratyaya-sāra*—that whose sole essence is the uninterrupted, self-certifying awareness of the one Self—and as *prapañcopaśamam* (the total quietude of phenomenal multiplicity), peaceful (*śāntam*), auspicious (*śivam*), and non-dual (*advaitam*).
