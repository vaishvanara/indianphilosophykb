Meaning: "the fourth"; the non-dual Absolute Consciousness (*Turīya*) beyond and underlying the three empirical states.

See: [Mandukya Upanishad (Verse 7, 12)](mandukyopanisat.md)

---

## Definition

Literally signifying "the fourth" (the ordinal numeral following the third), *caturthaḥ* in Vedantic text tradition is used synonymously with *turīya* or *turya*. Contextually in the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*, the term does not designate a temporal sequence, a fourth numerical state of mind, or a fourth part added to three pre-existing parts. Rather, it denotes the unconditioned, non-dual Reality (*Ātman* / *Brahman*) that serves as the eternal witness and substratum (*āspada*) for the three empirical quarters (*pādas*): waking (*viśva*), dream (*taijasa*), and deep sleep (*prājña*). 

While the first three quarters represent phenomenal manifestations subject to cause and effect or causal ignorance, *caturthaḥ* is defined through radical negation (*neti neti*) as relationless (*avyavahārya*), unperceived (*adṛṣṭa*), peaceful (*śānta*), blissful (*śiva*), and non-dual (*advaita*). Idiomatically, it is designated as "the fourth" solely because it is expounded after the three empirical states have been analyzed and sublated.

---

## Etymology

The masculine nominal form *caturthaḥ* (Sanskrit: चतुर्थः; neuter *caturtham*: चतुर्थम्) is an ordinal numeral derived from the cardinal numeral stem *catur* (चतुर्), meaning "four". Grammatically, the ordinal suffix *-tha* (Paninian *-tha* / *-ṭhan*) is appended to the base *catur* to form *caturtha* ("fourth"). In Vedic and Classical Sanskrit texts, the older Vedic variant *turīya* (तुरीय) or *turya* (तूर्य) functions as an exact semantic and etymological equivalent of *caturtha*, both designating the fourth element in a series. 

In textual passages such as *Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad* 5.14.3, the text explicitly equates the two terms, observing that what is called *turīya* is identical in meaning to *caturtha* (*turīyam iti caturtham*). Vedantic commentators emphasize that although the etymological origin is strictly numerical, philosophical doctrine cannot be inferred from etymology alone, as *caturthaḥ* in Vedanta signifies transcendental non-duality rather than a numerical tally.

## Caturthaḥ in the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad

In the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*, the term occurs in verse 7 as the accusative singular noun/adjective *caturtham* (चतुर्थम्) and in verse 12 as the nominative singular *caturthaḥ* (चतुर्थः). In verse 7, *caturtham* serves as the predicate of the sentence describing the transcendental nature of the Self after negating all subjective, objective, and cognitive attributes (*nāntaḥ-prajñaṁ na bahiḥ-prajñam...*). The surrounding text structures human experience into four quarters (*catuṣpāt*), wherein the first three quarters, namely waking (*viśva*), dream (*taijasa*), and deep sleep (*prājña*), represent limited aspects of consciousness. Verse 12 further correlates *caturthaḥ* with the soundless/partless (*amātra*) measure of AUM, declaring that one who knows this fourth aspect merges the individual self into the supreme Self (*saṁviśaty ātmanāttmānaṁ ya evaṁ veda*).

---

### Textual strata and school-specific interpretations

Vedantic literature establishes a clear distinction between the core prose *Upaniṣad*, Gauḍapāda's verse commentary (*Kārikā*), and Śaṅkarācārya's exposition:

- **Upaniṣadic text**: The prose verses concisely present *caturthaḥ* as the non-dual Self (*Ātman*), completely free from empirical attributes and identical with the soundless AUM (*amātra*).
- **Gauḍapāda Kārikā**: In the *Āgama Prakaraṇa* (I.10–16), Gauḍapāda equates *caturthaḥ* (or *turīya*) with the changeless, effulgent Lord (*īśana*) who is devoid of both cause (*kāraṇa*) and effect (*kārya*). Gauḍapāda stresses that while waking and dream are bound by mis-apprehension (*svapna*) and deep sleep by non-apprehension (*nidrā*), *caturthaḥ* is free from both, being eternal light (*sarvadṛk*) and non-origination (*ajāti*).
- **Śaṅkarācārya's Advaita commentary**: Śaṅkara clarifies that *caturthaḥ* is not a literal fourth state added sequentially to the three states of waking, dream, and deep sleep. Instead, it is the non-relational substratum (*akhaṇḍārtha*) revealed when the illusory attributes (*upādhi*) of the three states are sublated through knowledge (*adhyāropa-apavāda*).
- **Later Vedantic and Neo-Vedantic perspectives**: In treatises such as the *Pañcadaśī* of Vidyāraṇya and *Vedāntasāra* of Sadānanda, *caturthaḥ* / *Turīya* is defined as the pure, unattached witness consciousness (*sākṣin*) that underlies both collective and individual ignorance. In the teachings of Ramana Maharshi, *Turīya* is described as "wakeful sleep" (*jāgrat-suṣupti*); Ramana Maharshi notes that because the three empirical states are ultimately unreal, *Turīya* is not merely a fourth state but the only real state, often designated as *Turīyātīta* ("transcending the fourth").

---

## Primary texts and commentaries

Primary textual locators and commentary passages establishing the term *caturthaḥ* include:

- **Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad, verse 7**: Formulates the primary definition of *caturthaḥ* through negative theology (*neti neti*), identifying it as *śāntaṁ śivam advaitaṁ caturthaṁ manyante sa ātmā sa vijñeyaḥ* ("Peaceful, auspicious, and non-dual is the fourth thought to be; that is the Self, that is to be known").
- **Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad, verse 12**: Equates *caturthaḥ* with the soundless/partless (*amātra*) AUM, stating *amātraś caturtho 'vyavahāryaḥ prapañcopaśamaḥ śivo 'dvaita evam oṁkāra ātmaiva* ("The partless fourth is beyond empirical dealings, the cessation of the phenomenal world, auspicious, and non-dual. Thus, the syllable Om is verily the Self").
- **Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad, Adhyāya V, Brāhmaṇa 14, verse 3**: Establishes the equivalence between *Turīya* and *caturtha* in the context of the Gāyatrī meter: *yāvai tasyaitāvat turīyaṁ darśataṁ padaṁ parorajā ya eṣa tapati, yad vai turīyaṁ iti caturthaṁ tat* ("Verily, that fourth visible foot of it is this (sun) that shines beyond the realm of *rajas*/dust; indeed, that which is '*Turīya*' is '*caturtha*' (the fourth)").
- **Gauḍapāda Kārikā, Āgama Prakaraṇa (Book I), verses 10–16, 29**: Analyzes *Turīya* / *caturtha* as the non-dual ruler (*īśana*) free from cause and effect, contrastable with *Viśva*, *Taijasa*, and *Prājña*.
- **Śaṅkarācārya's Māṇḍūkyopaniṣad-bhāṣya (on verses 7 and 12)**: Explains the methodology of sublation (*apavāda*) and demonstrates that *caturthaḥ* is the changeless witness of the three states.
- **Sadānanda's Vedāntasāra, verse 5**: Defines the transcendental fourth aspect of consciousness (*Turīya*) as the substratum of collective and individual ignorance.

## Interpretations and Disputes

Significant textual and philosophical discussions regarding *caturthaḥ* in the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* center on several key issues:

1. **Numerical state vs. substratum of reality**: A common misinterpretation treats *caturthaḥ* as a fourth sequential state of consciousness alongside waking, dreaming, and deep sleep. Advaita Vedānta commentators, led by Śaṅkara, reject this view, arguing that if *caturthaḥ* were merely a fourth temporal state, it would imply voidness (*śūnyatā*) or transience; instead, it is the eternal, non-relational substratum (*sākṣin*) present throughout all states.
2. **Status of deep sleep (*Prājña*) vs. *caturthaḥ***: Commentators debate whether the bliss experienced in deep sleep (*Prājña*) constitutes ultimate *Brahman*. Śaṅkara notes that deep sleep is conditioned by the causal seed of ignorance (*bīja* / *ajñāna*), whereas *caturthaḥ* alone is entirely free from both causal seed and illusory projection.
3. **Textual unity and Buddhist parallels**: Modern scholars, such as Vidhusekhara Bhattacharya and Karl H. Potter,  document historical debates concerning the relationship between the prose *Upaniṣad* verses and Gauḍapāda's *Kārikā*. While non-Advaitic traditions sometimes treated both prose and verse as scriptural *śruti*, Advaita tradition maintains that the prose text is the original *Upaniṣad* and the *Kārikā* are Gauḍapāda's commentary. Scholars also analyze Gauḍapāda's use of terminology in Book IV (*Alātāśānti Prakaraṇa*), noting discussions over Buddhist vocabulary versus classical Vedantic non-duality.
4. **School-specific philosophical differences**: While Advaita Vedānta interprets *caturthaḥ* as absolute, non-dual Consciousness (*Advaita*), Viśiṣṭādvaita and Dvaita traditions interpret references to the supreme Reality as designating the personal Supreme Lord (*Īśvara* / *Viṣṇu*) endowed with real divine attributes, maintaining real distinctions between the individual soul (*jīva*) and the Supreme.
