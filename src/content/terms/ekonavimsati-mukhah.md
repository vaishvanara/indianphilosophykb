Meaning: "nineteen mouthed;" having nineteen mouths or instruments of experience, characterizing the waking and dream states.

See: [Mandukya Upanishad (Verses 3 and 4)](mandukyopanisat.md)

---

## Definition

The Sanskrit compound *ekonaviṃśati-mukhaḥ* (एकोनविंशतिमुखः) is composed of two primary elements: the compound cardinal numeral *ekonaviṃśati* (nineteen) and the noun stem *mukha* (mouth, face, aperture, or entrance). Taken literally, the expression translates as "having nineteen mouths".

In the context of Upanishadic philosophy and Vedantic exegesis, "mouth" (*mukha*) is used figuratively to denote an avenue of perception, gateway of cognition, or instrument of experience (*upalabdhi-dvāra*) through which the experiencing subject interacts with objects. 

In his commentary on the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*, Śaṅkarācārya enumerates the nineteen instruments (*ekonaviṃśati-mukha*) that constitute this cognitive and active apparatus:

1. **The five organs of perception (*jñānendriya* or *buddhindriya*):** hearing/ear (*śrotra*), touch/skin (*tvac*), sight/eye (*cakṣus*), taste/tongue (*rasana*), and smell/nose (*ghrāṇa*).
2. **The five organs of action (*karmendriya*):** speech (*vāc*), grasping/hands (*pāṇi*), locomotion/feet (*pāda*), excretion/anus (*pāyu*), and generation/genitals (*upastha*).
3. **The five vital breaths or life-forces (*pañca-prāṇas*):** *prāṇa*, *apāna*, *vyāna*, *samāna*, and *udāna*.
4. **The four aspects of the inner organ (*antaḥkaraṇa-catuṣṭaya*):** the deliberative mind (*manas*), the determining intellect (*buddhi*), egoity (*ahaṃkāra*), and the memory/mind-stuff (*citta*).

Together, these nineteen instruments form the complete subtle apparatus through which consciousness experiences gross material objects in the waking state and subtle mental impressions in the dream state.

## Etymology

Grammatically, *ekonaviṃśati-mukhaḥ* (Sanskrit: एकोनविंशतिमुखः) is an adjectival compound belonging to the class of *Bahuvrīhi* (possessive or exocentric compounds). It is inflected in the nominative singular masculine form to modify the macrocosmic and microcosmic manifestations of the Self (*Ātman*).

The compound consists of two main stems:

- ***ekonaviṃśati* (एकोनविंशति):** A compound numeral formed from *eka* (one) + *ūna* (less by / lacking) + *viṃśati* (twenty), literally meaning "twenty less one," i.e., "nineteen".
- ***mukha* (मुख):** A neuter nominal stem meaning "mouth," "face," "entrance," or "instrument".

The traditional analytical expansion (*vigraha-vākya*) of this *Bahuvrīhi* compound is:

- *ekonaviṃśatir mukhāni yasya saḥ = ekonaviṃśati-mukhaḥ* ("He whose mouths or avenues of perception are nineteen in number").

In the text of the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*, the compound stem *ekonaviṃśati-mukha* takes the masculine nominative singular inflection *-ḥ*, agreeing in gender, number, and case with *vaiśvānaraḥ* in verse 3 and *Taijasaḥ* in verse 4.

## Ekonaviṃśati-mukhaḥ in the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad

The compound *ekonaviṃśati-mukhaḥ* appears twice in the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*, occurring in both verse 3 and verse 4:

1. **Verse 3 (the waking state / *Vaiśvānara*)**:
   > *jāgaritasthāno bahihprajñaḥ saptāṅga ekonaviṃśatimukhaḥ sthūlabhug vaiśvānaraḥ prathamaḥ pādaḥ // 3 //*
   > "The first quarter is *Vaiśvānara*, whose sphere of activity is the waking state, who is conscious of external objects, who has seven limbs and nineteen mouths, and who experiences gross objects."

2. **Verse 4 (the dream state / *Taijasa*)**:
   > *svapnasthāno'ntaḥprajñaḥ saptāṅga ekonaviṃśatimukhaḥ praviviktabhuk taijaso dvitīyaḥ pādaḥ // 4 //*
   > "The second quarter is *Taijasa*, whose sphere of activity is the dream state, who is conscious of internal objects, who has seven limbs and nineteen mouths, and who experiences subtle objects."

Grammatically, *ekonaviṃśati-mukhaḥ* functions in both verses as a nominative singular masculine adjectival modifier.

### Philosophical significance and contextual interpretation

- **Waking and dream parallelism**: The *Upaniṣad* assigns the exact same nineteen instruments (*ekonaviṃśati-mukhaḥ*) to both *Vaiśvānara* (waking) and *Taijasa* (dream). In the waking state (*jāgarita-sthāna*), these nineteen instruments interact with external physical objects (*sthūla-bhuk*). In the dream state (*svapna-sthāna*), when external senses are inactive, the mind uses internal impressions (*vāsanā*) left by waking experiences, so that *Taijasa* experiences subtle objects (*pravivikta-bhuk*) through the same nineteen avenues of cognition operating internally.
- **Contrast with deep sleep (*suṣupti*)**: In verse 5, describing the third quarter (*Prājña*), the nineteen individual mouths are no longer active. Instead, all experiences become unified (*ekī-bhūtaḥ*) into a single mass of consciousness (*prajñāna-ghanaḥ*), demonstrating that the nineteen mouths belong to the realm of dualistic manifestation.

### Exegetical distinctions

- ***Upaniṣad* wording**: The *Upaniṣad* presents *ekonaviṃśati-mukhaḥ* as a structural attribute defining the structural complexity of the first two quarters of *Ātman*.
- **Gauḍapāda's *Kārikā***: Gauḍapāda (I.1–I.5) synthesizes the cosmic and individual dimensions of *Viśva* and *Taijasa*, explaining that while *Viśva* cognizes external objects through the physical senses, *Taijasa* cognizes subtle forms within the internal mind, establishing that both are bound by cause and effect (*kāryakāraṇabaddha*).
- **Śaṅkara's commentary**: Śaṅkara provides the explicit 19-fold classification (five *jñānendriya*, five *karmendriya*, five *prāṇa*, and four *antaḥkaraṇa*), clarifying that *mukha* signifies the instruments or gateways through which consciousness appears to experience objects due to ignorance (*avidyā*).

## Interpretations and Disputes

### Variations in the classification of *antaḥ-karaṇa*

While Śaṅkara's commentary on *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* verse 3 explicitly enumerates four distinct functions of the inner organ (*manas*, *buddhi*, *ahaṃkāra*, and *citta*) to arrive at the count of nineteen, other Vedantic and Sāṃkhya texts group *antaḥkaraṇa* into two (*manas* and *buddhi*) or three (*manas*, *buddhi*, and *ahaṃkāra*), yielding seventeen items for the subtle body (*sūkṣma-śarīra* or *liṅga-śarīra*). In the *Māṇḍūkya* context, commentators emphasize that *ahaṃkāra* (egoity) and *citta* (memory/recollection) are listed separately from *manas* and *buddhi* specifically to complete the nineteen distinct instruments of experience.

### Literal vs. metaphorical readings

Early modern translations sometimes render *ekonaviṃśati-mukhaḥ* literally as "having nineteen mouths". Classical commentators stress that this is symbolic and functional: the "mouths" represent the nineteen functional channels through which the experiencing subject "swallows" or enjoys (*bhoktṛtva*) gross and subtle sense-objects.
