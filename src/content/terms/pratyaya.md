Meaning: “condition;” the cognition or conviction of the single Self (*ekātman*), forming the essence of non-dual realization.

See: [Mandukya Upanishad (Verse 7)](mandukyopanisat.md)

## Definition

In Sanskrit literature and philosophical discourse, *pratyaya* (प्रत्यय) broadly signifies a mental conviction, notion, idea, cause, confidence, or grammatical suffix. In the specific context of the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* (mantra 7), the term functions as a vital constituent of the compound expression *ekātma-pratyaya-sāram* (एकात्मप्रत्ययसारम्). The constituent words of this compound are *eka* (one or single), *ātman* (the Self), *pratyaya* (cognition, awareness, or conviction), and *sāra* (essence, substrate, or sole evidence). Combined, the expression designates that ultimate reality—*Turīya* (the Fourth)—whose sole essence or valid proof consists in the unbroken cognition and conviction of the non-dual Self. Literally, *pratyaya* means a going towards, turning to, or taking recourse to an object or idea. Idiomatically, it spans subjective belief (*sampratyaya*), conceptual impression, causal dependence (*pratyayatva*), and grammatical affixes. Contextually within Advaita Vedānta, it functions as the inward mental apprehension that leads to the sublation of illusory duality.

---

## Etymology

Grammatically, *pratyaya* (प्रत्यय) is derived from the Sanskrit prefix *prati-* (प्रति, meaning "towards", "against", or "counter") combined with the verbal root *i* (इ, meaning "to go") and the nominal suffix *a* (*ghañ* or *ac*), literally denoting "that which goes towards", "recourse", or "conviction". 

In the compound *ekātma-pratyaya-sāram* (एकात्मप्रत्ययसारम्), the word formation operates in three stages:
- *ekātman* (एकात्मन्): A Karmadhāraya compound formed from *eka* ("single" or "one") and *ātman* ("Self"), denoting "the single Self".
- *ekātma-pratyaya* (एकात्मप्रत्यय): A Genitive Tatpuruṣa compound (*ekātmanaḥ pratyayaḥ*), signifying "the cognition, awareness, or conviction of the single Self".
- *ekātma-pratyaya-sāram* (एकात्मप्रत्ययसारम्): A Bahuvrīhi compound (*ekātma-pratyayaḥ sāraḥ yasya tat*), expanded as "that entity whose sole essence or evidence (*sāra*) is the conviction of the single Self".

## Pratyaya in the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad

The term occurs in Mantra 7 of the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*, where it describes *Turīya*, the transcendent Fourth state of Consciousness beyond waking (*viśva*), dream (*taijasa*), and deep sleep (*prājña*). Grammatically, *ekātma-pratyaya-sāram* functions as an adjectival modifier qualifying *Turīya*. Contextually, Mantra 7 defines *Turīya* through a series of negative and positive attributes: it is neither inward-conscious (*nāntaḥ-prajñam*) nor outward-conscious (*na bahis-prajñam*), unseen (*adṛṣṭam*), ungraspable (*agrāhyam*), unthinkable (*acintyam*), the cessation of phenomenal multiplicity (*prapañcopaśamam*), tranquil (*śāntam*), auspicious (*śivam*), and non-dual (*advaitam*). 

Within this surrounding text, *ekātma-pratyaya-sāram* serves as the pivotal positive indicator of the Self. In his commentary (*Bhāṣya*), Śaṅkarācārya explains that *Turīya* is established through the unbroken cognition of the identity of the single Self (*ekātma-pratyaya*), which constitutes its sole valid proof and essence. Just as the true nature of a rope is realized by negating the illusory appearance of a snake, the non-dual Self present across all three states is recognized as *Turīya* by negating illusory *upādhis* through *ekātma-pratyaya*. Gauḍapāda's *Kārikā* further clarifies that while deep sleep (*prājña*) is conditioned by non-apprehension of reality as a cause (*bīja-nidrā*), *Turīya* is ever-seeing (*sarvadṛk*) and untouched by causal ignorance or dreamlike mis-apprehension.

## Pratyaya in Sāṃkhya

In the classical Sāṃkhya tradition, *pratyaya* (प्रत्यय) functions as a foundational epistemological, psychological, and cosmological concept. While derived etymologically from the Sanskrit verbal root *i* prefixed with *prati*—signifying a going toward, conviction, trust, or mental representation—Sāṃkhya elevates the term into a rigorous technical designation for mental operations and the manifest categories of empirical experience.

In primary textual commentary, such as the anonymous *Yuktidīpikā* on Īśvarakṛṣṇa's *Sāṃkhyakārikā* (verses 46–51), *pratyaya* is explicitly identified as synonymous with key cognitive and ontological terms. As recorded in the *Yuktidīpikā* summary compiled by Gerald J. Larson and Ram Shankar Bhattacharya in the *Encyclopedia of Indian Philosophies* (Volume 4), *pratyaya* is treated as equivalent to *padārtha* (category or subsidiary category), *lakṣaṇa* (distinguishing characteristic), *buddhi* (intellect), *niścaya* (certainty), and *adhyavasāya* (ascertainment or cognitive determination). Consequently, the compound *pratyayasarga* is synonymous with *padārthasarga* and *lakṣaṇasarga*, denoting the intellectual creation—the structured emergence of empirical cognition and phenomenal experience originating from the intellect (*buddhi*).

The Sāṃkhya doctrine of *pratyayasarga* (intellectual creation) enumerates fifty categories (*padārtha*s) that constitute the subjective and psychological manifold of worldly existence. As presented in Vācaspati Miśra's *Tattvakaumudī* and further elaborated in the *Yuktidīpikā*, these fifty intellectual categories are systematically classified into four main divisions:

1. **Five Misconceptions (*viparyaya*)**: The fundamental cognitive errors or ignorance (*avidyā*) dominated by inertia (*tamas*). In Sāṃkhya textual commentary, these five misconceptions are designated by archaic or traditional terms—*tamas* (obscuration), *moha* (delusion), *mahāmoha* (great delusion), *tāmisra* (gloom), and *andhatāmisra* (utter darkness)—which directly correlate with the five afflictions (*kleśa*s: *avidyā*, *asmitā*, *rāga*, *dveṣa*, and *abhiniveśa*) articulated in Pātañjala Yoga.
2. **Twenty-Eight Dysfunctions or Incapacities (*aśakti*)**: Functional impairments comprising eleven injuries to the capacities (the five sensory capacities, five motor capacities, and the mind or *manas*) along with seventeen dysfunctions of the intellect itself.
3. **Nine Contentments or Acquiescences (*tuṣṭi*)**: States of mental satisfaction or complacency—four internal (*ādhyātmika*) and five external (*bāhya*)—wherein a seeker mistakes partial quietude or temporary natural absorption for final liberation.
4. **Eight Attainments or Perfections (*siddhi*)**: Rational capacities and modes of intellectual progress through which radical discernment is achieved, comprising reasoning (*ūha*), oral instruction (*śabda*), study (*adhyayana*), the three ways of overcoming frustration (*duḥkhavighātāḥ trayaḥ*), cultivation of friends (*suhṛtprāpti*), and purity/generosity (*dāna*).

In modern scholarship, Gerald J. Larson emphasizes that *pratyayasarga* represents the fundamental Sāṃkhya interpretation of the phenomenal, empirical world of ordinary life, which must be clearly distinguished from the noumenal or causal realm of the twenty-five principles (*tattva*s). Rather than an peripheral scheme, *pratyayasarga* establishes Sāṃkhya as a critical realism. While the *tattva*s lay out the noumenal material and spiritual constituents of reality (*prakṛti* and *puruṣa*), *pratyayasarga* maps the cognitive, ethical, and dispositional structure through which living beings experience and navigate worldly existence (*saṃsāra*).

Furthermore, in Sāṃkhya epistemology as articulated by Vijñānabhikṣu in his *Sāṃkhyapravacanabhāṣya* (under Sūtra I.64 and I.100), *pratyaya* describes the actual modification (*vṛtti*) of the intellect (*buddhi*). Cognition occurs when the intellect takes on the form of the external object (*arthākāra*). Because *puruṣa* (pure consciousness) is unattached (*asaṅga*) and actionless (*niṣkriya*), consciousness appears to experience objects through the reflection (*pratibimba*) of the intellectual *pratyaya* in *puruṣa*, or through the reflection of *puruṣa* in the luminous *sattva* constituent of the intellect.

## Pratyaya in Rāja Yoga

In the *Yogasūtra* of Patañjali and its classic commentaries, *pratyaya* serves as a central technical term denoting the mental content, idea, presentation, thought-state, or total visual/conceptual field occupying consciousness (*citta*) at any given moment.

In I. K. Taimni's analytical exposition *The Science of Yoga*, *pratyaya* is defined as the total content of the mind that occupies the field of consciousness at a particular time, encompassing sensory images, abstract concepts, memories, or meditative objects across all planes of manifestation. Patañjali categorizes mental modifications (*vṛtti*s) not only by their affective quality (painful or non-painful, *kliṣṭa* / *akliṣṭa*) but also by the nature of the *pratyaya* generated within the mind.

The doctrinal role of *pratyaya* is pivotal across the stages of meditative concentration (*samādhi*):

1. **Cognitive Concentration (*samprajñāta-samādhi*)**: In *samprajñāta-samādhi*, consciousness remains directed outward toward a specific object or mental image, termed a "seed" (*sabīja*) or *pratyaya*. The mind maintains continuous contact with this intentional content through successive stages of reflection (*vitarka*), deliberation (*vicāra*), joy (*ānanda*), and I-am-ness (*asmitā*).
2. **Non-Cognitive Concentration (*asamprajñāta-samādhi*)**: Defined in *Yogasūtra* I.18 (*virāma-pratyayābhyāsa-pūrvaḥ saṃskāra-śeṣo 'nyaḥ*), this unseeded state is attained through the deliberate practice of the cessation (*virāma*) of all *pratyaya*s. When the intentional image or *pratyaya* is dropped, consciousness recedes back toward its source, leaving only residual latent impressions (*saṃskāra-śeṣa*).
3. **Meditative Absorption (*dhyāna*)**: In *Yogasūtra* III.2 (*tatra pratyayaika-tānatā dhyānam*), Patañjali defines *dhyāna* as the single, continuous, and uninterrupted flow of perception (*pratyaya*) toward the chosen object within the region of concentration (*deśa*).
4. **Inter-Mental Cognition and Telepathy**: In *Yogasūtra* III.19 (*pratyayasya paracitta-jñānam*), Patañjali states that through *saṃyama* (combined concentration, meditation, and absorption) on the *pratyaya* (mental representation or image) of another person, a yogi gains direct knowledge of that person's mind.
5. **Discriminative Discernment (*viveka-khyāti*)**: In *Yogasūtra* III.35 (or III.36 in some recensions), Patañjali explains that experience (*bhoga*) results from the failure to distinguish between the intellect's *sattva* constituent and the *puruṣa*, which are utterly distinct. By performing *saṃyama* on the distinction between the *pratyaya* of *sattva* (which operates for another's purpose, *parārtha*) and the *puruṣa* (which exists for itself, *svārtha*), true knowledge of the *puruṣa* is unveiled.
6. **Oscillation in Advanced Practice**: In *Yogasūtra* IV.27 (*tacchidreṣu pratyayāntarāṇi saṃskārebhyaḥ*), Patañjali notes that during the intervals or gaps (*chidra*) of discriminative insight, other competing *pratyaya*s (mental contents) emerge due to the residual force of past latent impressions (*saṃskāra*s).

Vyāsa's *Yogasūtrabhāṣya* and Vācaspati Miśra's *Tattvavaiśāradī* clarify that while ordinary *pratyaya*s are transitory modifications of the mind dependent upon objects, the ultimate aim of Rāja Yoga is to transcend all *pratyaya*s, dissolving the mind's identification with mental content (*citta-vṛtti-nirodha*) so that pure consciousness abides in its essential nature (*svarūpe 'vasthānam*).

## Pratyaya in Nyāya

In the Nyāya school of classical Indian logic and epistemology, as founded in Gautama's *Nyāyasūtra* and explicated in Vātsyāyana's *Nyāyasūtrabhāṣya*, Uddyotakara's *Nyāyavārtika*, and Ganganatha Jha's modern translations and annotations, *pratyaya* is consistently employed as a technical synonym for **cognition** (*jñāna*), **perception** (*pratyakṣa*), or **ascertainment** (*adhyavasāya* / *saṃpratyaya*).

In the opening sections of the *Nyāyasūtrabhāṣya* (under Sūtra 1.1.4), Vātsyāyana establishes that valid cognition (*pramā*) is produced through the operation of instruments of right knowledge (*pramāṇa*). Perception (*pratyakṣa-pratyaya*) is defined as cognition produced by the contact of a sense organ with its object (*indriyārtha-sannikarṣa-janya-jñāna*). Vātsyāyana and Uddyotakara emphasize that a true *pratyaya* (as *adhyavasāya*) excludes both doubtful cognition (*saṃśaya*) and erroneous cognition (*viparyaya*), carrying the character of definite, decisive apprehension.

In the extended polemics of *Nyāyasūtra* Discourse II (Sūtras 2.1.11–16), Gautama and Vātsyāyana address the temporal relationship between the instrument of cognition (*pramāṇa*) and the object of cognition (*prameya*). Opponents argue that a *pramāṇa* cannot precede, coincide with, or follow its object. In response, the Naiyāyikas clarify that the term *pratyaya* (signifying cognition or apprehension) applies across all three divisions of time (*traikālya*): past (*cognized*), present (*cognizing*), and future (*will be cognized*).

Furthermore, in Nyāya textual commentary (such as Raghuttama's *Bhāṣyacandra* cited in Ganganatha Jha's edition under Sūtra 2.1.33), the word *pratyaya* is frequently glossed directly as *pratyakṣa* (direct sensory awareness or perception). In logical debates regarding the reality of whole entities (*avayavin*) versus mere clusters of atoms, the Naiyāyikas argue that our uncontradicted, direct awareness (*pratyaya*) of composite objects (such as a jar or cloth) proves the objective existence of the composite entity over and above its constituent parts.

## Pratyaya in Vyākaraṇa

In the indigenous tradition of Sanskrit grammar (*Vyākaraṇa*)—spanning Pāṇini's *Aṣṭādhyāyī*, Kātyāyana's *Vārtika*s, Patañjali's *Mahābhāṣya*, Bhartṛhari's *Vākyapadīya*, and Kauṇḍa Bhaṭṭa's *Vaiyākaraṇabhūṣaṇasāra*—*pratyaya* is a technical structural term meaning **suffix** or **affix**.

Grammatically, a Sanskrit word (*pada*) is formed by affixing a *pratyaya* to a verbal root (*dhātu*) or nominal stem (*prātipadika*). Pāṇini organizes suffixal derivation across major sections of the *Aṣṭādhyāyī* (governed by the heading sūtra *pratyayaḥ* 3.1.1). Suffixes are broadly classified into:

1. **Primary Suffixes (*kṛt* / *kṛdanta*)**: Suffixes added directly to verbal roots to form nouns, adjectives, or indeclinable participles (e.g., adding *-ṇvucl* / *-aka* to the root *pac* yields *pācaka*, "cook"; adding *-ktvā* yields *paktvā*, "having cooked").
2. **Secondary Suffixes (*taddhita* / *taddhitānta*)**: Suffixes added to nominal stems to derive secondary nouns, patronymics, or abstract qualities (e.g., adding *-a* or *-ya* to *garga* yields *gārgya*).
3. **Abstract Suffixes (*bhāvapratyaya*)**: Suffixes such as *-tva* and *-tal* (prescribed under Pāṇini 5.1.119) that express the abstract quality, state, or ground for applying a word (*pravṛttinimitta*). For example, adding *-tva* to *go* (cow) yields *gotva* (cowness or the universal of cow).

A major semantic debate between Grammarians and Logicians (Naiyāyikas) concerns the relative predominance of the suffix meaning (*pratyayārtha*) versus the root/stem meaning (*prakṛtyārtha*). As Kauṇḍa Bhaṭṭa discusses in *Vaiyākaraṇabhūṣaṇasāra* (Section 9 & 13), the traditional grammatical rule dictates that the meaning conveyed by the suffix is predominant (*pratyayārtha-prādhānyam*). In a word like *pācaka* ("cook"), the *kṛt* suffix denotes the agent (*kartṛ*), qualifying the root action *pac* (cooking). Conversely, in finite verbal forms (*ākhyāta*), Grammarians hold that durative action (*bhāva* / *kriyā*) expressed by the root remains the principal element of sentence meaning, contesting the Nyāya view that nominative agent nouns are always principal.

In Bhartṛhari's philosophy of language (*Vākyapadīya*), while suffixes (*pratyaya*s) and stems are analyzed by grammarians through agreement and difference (*anvaya-vyatireka*) for instructional purposes, ultimate linguistic reality resides in the undivided sentence-burst (*vākya-sphoṭa*). From the perspective of absolute reality, word parts and suffixes are analytical abstractions (*akalpita*) serving as pragmatic means (*upāya*) to reveal the unified meaning (*pratibhā*).

## Pratyaya in Buddhism

In both Theravāda and Mahāyāna Buddhist traditions, *pratyaya* (Pali: *paccaya*) is a fundamental doctrinal term meaning **condition**, **cause**, **requisite**, or **supporting factor**.

In the foundational doctrine of Dependent Origination (*Paṭiccasamuppāda* in Pali; *Pratītyasamuppāda* in Sanskrit), *paccaya* denotes the conditional dependency linking the twelve factors of existence (*nidāna*s). The formula explicitly states: *avijjā-paccayā saṅkhārā* ("conditioned by ignorance arise mental formations"), *saṅkhāra-paccayā viññāṇaṃ* ("conditioned by mental formations arises consciousness"), continuing through birth, aging, and death.

Theravāda Abhidhamma expands conditional relations into a complex matrix in the *Paṭṭhāna* (the seventh book of the Abhidhamma Piṭaka). As detailed in Bhikkhu Bodhi's *Comprehensive Manual of Abhidhamma* and N. K. G. Mendis's *Abhidhamma in Practice*, the *Paṭṭhāna* establishes twenty-four conditional relations (*paccaya*s) that describe how mental and physical phenomena interact:

1. **Root Condition (*hetu-paccaya*)**: The six psychological roots—greed (*lobha*), hatred (*dosa*), delusion (*moha*), and their wholesome opposites—acting as roots for associated mental states.
2. **Object Condition (*ārammaṇa-paccaya*)**: Any sense-object or mental object that supports the arising of consciousness.
3. **Contiguity Condition (*anantara-paccaya*)**: The immediate cessation of a preceding thought-moment (*citta*) conditioning the arising of the subsequent thought-moment.
4. **Co-nascence Condition (*sahajāta-paccaya*)**: Phenomena arising simultaneously that condition one another (such as mental factors arising together with consciousness).
5. **Presence Condition (*atthi-paccaya*)**: Existing phenomena that condition other phenomena by their presence.

In Theravāda scholasticism, Ācariya Buddhaghosa in the *Visuddhimagga* and Buddhadatta in the *Abhidhammāvatāra* carefully distinguish a root cause (*hetu*) from a condition (*paccaya*). A *hetu* is the primary seed or root (such as wholesome or unwholesome motives), whereas a *paccaya* is any supportive factor that assists or maintains the arising (*uppādeti*) and continuation of a state (*dhamma*).

In Mahāyāna scriptures, particularly the *Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra* (translated and edited by Daisetz Teitaro Suzuki), *pratyaya* is examined within the framework of the Three Natures (*trisvabhāva*). The relative or dependent nature (*paratantra-svabhāva*) arises from subject-object conditioning (*hetu-pratyaya-ja*). Mahāyāna texts emphasize that all dependently originated phenomena (*pratītyasamutpanna*) lack intrinsic self-existence (*svabhāva*) and are fundamentally empty (*śūnya*). Spiritual liberation consists in transcending attachment to causal conditions (*hetu-pratyaya*) and false conceptualization (*vikalpa*), realizing the perfected, unconditioned nature (*pariniṣpanna-svabhāva*) and the great void of noble wisdom (*paramārtha-āryajñāna-mahāśūnyatā*).

## Interpretations and disputes

Philosophical traditions interpret *pratyaya* differently depending on their epistemological and ontological frameworks

- **Yogācāra Idealism vs. Advaita Vedānta**: In Buddhist Yogācāra philosophy, *pratyaya* denotes momentary mental impressions or causal streams of consciousness (*pratyaya-sarga* / *pratyaya-paramparā*) within the *ālaya-vijñāna*. In contrast, Advaita Vedānta treats empirical mental states (*vṛtti-pratyaya*) as transitory attributes of the intellect (*buddhi*) rather than the eternal Reality (*astīti pratyayo yaśca yaśca nāstīti vastuni*).
- **Role of Cognition in Liberation**: While Nyāya and Mīmāṁsā view *pratyaya* as discrete acts of knowledge producing empirical apprehension, Advaita Vedānta holds that the *ekātma-pratyaya* in the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* is the final sublating cognition that dispels nescience. Later Advaita texts like the *Vivekacūḍāmaṇi* emphasize that the ultimate state of liberation is *pratyayāntara-śūnya*—devoid of any intervening dual thoughts or concepts—and *pratyayāgamyamāna*, being beyond the reach of ordinary mental cognitions.
