Meaning: "the Self in the dream state, experiencing internal, subtle mental impressions through light-like mind-stuff."

See: [Mandukya Upanishad (Verse 4, 10)](mandukyopanisat.md)

---

## Definition

In the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* (verse 4), *Taijasa* (Sanskrit: तैजस) is defined as the second quarter (*dvitīyaḥ pādaḥ*) of the Self (*Ātman*). Its sphere of activity is the dream state (*svapna-sthāna*), and its consciousness is directed inward toward subjective mental states (*antaḥ-prajña*). The *Upaniṣad* describes *Taijasa* as possessing seven limbs (*saptāṅga*) and nineteen mouths (*ekonavimśati-mukha*), acting as the enjoyer of subtle objects (*pravivikta-bhuk*).

Literally, *Taijasa* means "consisting of light", "resplendent", or "luminous", derived from the Sanskrit noun *tejas* (light, brilliance, effulgence). Contextually, in Advaita Vedānta, *Taijasa* refers to consciousness conditioned by the subtle body (*sūkṣma-śarīra*) or inner organ (*antaḥ-karaṇa*). While the waking self (*Viśva*) perceives external physical objects through gross sensory channels, *Taijasa* functions when external senses are inactive. In this state, the mind projects a dream world out of the latent memory impressions (*vāsanā*) accumulated during waking experience, illuminated solely by the intrinsic effulgence of the Self.

---

## Etymology

The Sanskrit term *Taijasa* is a *taddhita* (secondary nominal) derivative formed from the neuter noun *tejas* (तेजस्), which denotes brilliance, fire, or light. The noun *tejas* itself derives from the verbal root √*tij* (तिज्), meaning "to be sharp", "to inflame", or "to shine". 

In Sanskrit word formation, *Taijasa* is produced by adding the secondary suffix *-a* (*aṇ* pratyaya) to *tejas*, which induces *vṛddhi* vowel-strengthening of the initial stem vowel *e* to *ai*. The resulting masculine stem *taijasa-* (nominative singular *taijasaḥ* तैजसः) carries the primary adjectival meaning of "luminous", "brilliant", or "composed of light". In Vedantic literature, this adjectival form is nominalized to signify the luminous consciousness operating within the subtle mental realm during dream.

## Taijasa in the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad

*Taijasa* appears explicitly in verse 4 of the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*:

> *svapnasthāno'ntaḥprajñaḥ saptāṅga ekonavimśatimukhaḥ praviviktabhuk taijasaḥ dvitīyaḥ pādaḥ*.

Grammatically, *taijasaḥ* functions as the nominative singular predicate identifying the second quarter (*dvitīyaḥ pādaḥ*) of *Ātman*. Contextually, the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* sets forth a fourfold structural analysis of reality and consciousness, mapping the four quarters of *Ātman* onto the sacred syllable *OM* (*A-U-M* and the soundless *amātra*). *Taijasa* corresponds to the second letter, *U* (*ukāra*). 

The *Upaniṣad* and its commentaries establish this identity on two grounds: "superiority" (*utkarṣa*), because subtle dream cognition transcends gross physical objects, and "being in the middle" (*ubhayatva* or *madhyavartitva*), as *Taijasa* stands intermediate between the waking state (*Viśva* / *A*) and deep sleep (*Prājña* / *M*).

The textual presentation of *Taijasa* develops across several historical layers in the Advaita tradition:

### Upanishadic wording

The *Upaniṣad* characterizes *Taijasa* through specific symbolic features: seven limbs (*saptāṅga*—the heaven as head, the sun as eye, the wind as breath, the sky as body, water as bladder, earth as feet, and fire as mouth) and nineteen mouths (*ekonavimśatimukha*—five organs of perception, five organs of action, five vital breaths, and the four internal faculties: mind, intellect, ego, and memory). Through these subtle instruments, *Taijasa* experiences impressions (*praviviktabhuk*).

### Gauḍapāda's Kārikā

Gauḍapāda opens his *Āgama Prakaraṇa* (GK 1.1) by defining *Taijasa* as *antaḥstho hyantaḥprajñaḥ* ("cognizer of internal objects within the body"). In subsequent verses (GK 1.11, 1.14–1.15), Gauḍapāda analyzes *Taijasa* ontologically alongside *Viśva*: both states are conditioned by cause (*kāraṇa*, non-apprehension of reality / *tattvāgrahaṇa*) and effect (*kārya*, mis-apprehension of reality / *anyathāgrahaṇa* or *svapna*). While *Prājña* is bound by non-apprehension alone, *Taijasa* actively engages in mis-apprehending non-dual reality through dream projections.

### Śaṅkara's commentary

Śaṅkara elucidates why the term *Taijasa* ("luminous") is employed. In dream, external luminaries (such as the sun) and external sense organs are inactive. The mind, retaining waking memory impressions (*vāsanās*), shines by the light of the Self (*svayaṁ-jyotiḥ*) and manifests as subject, object, and perception. Śaṅkara clarifies that *Taijasa* is essentially non-different from *Viśva*; it is the same perceiver that has withdrawn from external sense gates into the subtle space of the heart (*hṛdaya-ākāśa*).

### Later Vedantic prakaraṇa texts

In works such as the *Tattva Bodha*, *Vedāntasāra*, and *Pañcadaśī*, *Taijasa* is defined as *sūkṣma-śarīrābhimānī ātmā* (the Self identifying with the individual subtle body). Furthermore, Vedantic cosmology correlates microcosmic *Taijasa* with macrocosmic *Hiraṇyagarbha* or *Sūtrātmā* (the cosmic subtle intellect), preserving parallelism across individual and cosmic tiers (*Viśva*/*Virāṭ*, *Taijasa*/*Hiraṇyagarbha*, *Prājña*/*Īśvara*).

## Interpretations and disputes

Vedantic commentators discuss whether the objects perceived by *Taijasa* have objective reality. In his *Brahmasūtra-bhāṣya* (BS 3.2.1–3.2.6), Śaṅkara demonstrates that dream objects created during the *Taijasa* state are *māyāmātra* (mere illusion). They lack the spatial, temporal, and causal consistency of waking experience, even though they appear real to *Taijasa* during the dream itself.

While secondary Vedantic treatises treat waking experience (*Viśva*) as the generative cause of dream impressions (*Taijasa*), Gauḍapāda's *Alātaśānti Prakaraṇa* (GK 4.38–4.42) refutes absolute causality between the two states. Gauḍapāda contends that both waking and dream objects are mutually dependent mental constructions (*vikalpa*), neither of which possesses ultimate origination (*ajāti*).

Commentators debate whether dream perception by *Taijasa* is merely memory recall (*smṛti*) or a direct, immediate perception (*pratyakṣa*) of internal mental modifications. Advaita thinkers generally classify dream cognition as a unique direct perception of mental impressions (*vāsanā-mātra*) lit up by the witness-consciousness (*sākṣin*).
