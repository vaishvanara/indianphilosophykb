Meaning: "that is the Self" referring to *Turīya*, the non-dual, transcendent Reality to be realized.

See: [Mandukya Upanishad (Verse 7)](mandukyopanisat.md)

---

## Definition

The expression *sa ātmā* (Sanskrit: स आत्मा) is a two-word Sanskrit nominal phrase composed of the masculine demonstrative pronoun *sa* (स, "that" or "he") and the nominative singular noun *ātmā* (आत्मा, "Self" or "inmost essence"). Syntactically, the demonstrative pronoun *sa* functions as the subject referring back to the unconditioned Reality previously described, while *ātmā* acts as the predicate noun identifying that Reality with the intrinsic Self of the individual.

Literally translating to "That is the Self," the phrase carries a crucial contextual and idiomatic function in Vedantic discourse. Rather than indicating an external or distant object, *sa* points back to the transcendent, non-dual consciousness designated as the "Fourth" (*Turīya* or *caturtha*). Contextually, *sa ātmā* asserts the absolute identity between the ultimate, unconditioned Reality beyond empirical states and the innermost consciousness (*pratyag-ātman*) of the seeker.

## Etymology

The phrase *sa ātmā* demonstrates standard Sanskrit pronominal inflection and euphonic combination (*sandhi*):

1. ***Sa* (स):** The nominative singular masculine form of the demonstrative pronoun stem *tad* (तद्). Under standard Sanskrit grammatical rules governing visarga elision (e.g., Pāṇini 6.1.132: *etaṭtadoḥ sulopo koraṇañsamāse hali* and related Sandhi rules), the original nominative singular form *saḥ* (सः) regularly drops its final visarga (*-ḥ*) before all vowels except short *a-*, as well as before all consonants. Consequently, when *saḥ* precedes *ātmā*, the visarga is elided, resulting in *sa ātmā* (स आत्मा) with the hiatus between the two vowels preserved without vocalic coalescence.
2. ***Ātmā* (आत्मा):** The nominative singular masculine form of the n-stem noun *ātman* (आत्मन्). Etymologically, traditional Sanskrit grammarians derive *ātman* from roots such as *at* ("to move continuously"), *āp* ("to pervade" or "to reach"), or *an* ("to breathe"), designating that which continuously pervades, sustains, and animates individual existence.

Grammatically, the phrase constitutes an unexpressed-copula clause in which the present tense verb "is" (*asti*) is implied between the subject pronoun *sa* and the predicate noun *ātmā*.

## Sa ātmā in the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad

The phrase *sa ātmā* occurs at the climax of verse 7 of the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*, one of the principal Upaniṣads of the *Atharva Veda*. A closely related demonstrative construction, *so 'yam ātmā* (*saḥ ayam ātmā*, "this same Self"), also appears in verse 2 of the same text.

### Textual context and verse Location

In verse 7, the *Upaniṣad* defines the ultimate nature of Reality through a rigorous series of apophatic negations followed by positive characterizations, culminating in the declaration containing *sa ātmā*:

> नान्तःप्रज्ञं न बहिष्प्रज्ञं नोभयतस्प्रज्ञं न प्रज्ञानघनं न प्रज्ञं नाप्रज्ञम्। अदृष्टमव्यवहार्यमग्राह्यमलक्षणमचिन्त्यमव्यपदेश्यमेकात्मप्रत्ययसारं प्रपञ्चोपशमं शान्तं शिवमद्वैतं चतुर्थं मन्यन्ते स आत्मा स विज्ञेयः॥  
> 
> *nāntaḥprajñaṁ na bahiṣprajñaṁ nobhayataḥprajñaṁ na prajñānaghanaṁ na prajñaṁ nāprajñam | adṛṣṭam avyavahāryam agrāhyam alakṣaṇam acintyam avyapadeśyam ekātmapratyayasāraṁ prapañcopaśamaṁ śāntaṁ śivam advaitaṁ caturthaṁ manyante sa ātmā sa vijñeyaḥ ||*  
> 
> "They consider the Fourth (*turīya*) to be that which is not conscious of the internal world, nor conscious of the external world, nor conscious of both, nor a mass of consciousness, nor simple consciousness, nor unconsciousness. It is unperceived (*adṛṣya*), unrelated (*avyavahārya*), ungraspable (*agrāhya*), uninferable (*alakṣaṇa*), unthinkable (*acintya*), indescribable (*avyapadeśya*), the essence of the sole consciousness of the Self (*ekātmapratyayasāra*), the cessation of all phenomena (*prapañcopaśama*), peaceful (*śānta*), auspicious (*śiva*), and non-dual (*advaita*). **That is the Self (*sa ātmā**); **That is to be known (*sa vijñeyaḥ**)."

### Grammatical function and surrounding text

Within verse 7, *sa ātmā* functions as the primary syntactical assertion that synthesizes the preceding attributes. The *Upaniṣad* systematically negates the characteristics of the three empirical states of consciousness analyzed in verses 3–6:

- The waking state (*jāgarita-sthāna*), represented by *Viśva* / *Vaiśvānara*, which is conscious of external objects (*bahiṣprajña*).
- The dream state (*svapna-sthāna*), represented by *Taijasa*, which is conscious of internal subtle mental impressions (*antaḥprajña*).
- The deep sleep state (*suṣupti-sthāna*), represented by *Prājña*, which is a unified mass of dormant consciousness (*prajñāna-ghana*).

By declaring *caturthaṁ manyante sa ātmā sa vijñeyaḥ*, the *Upaniṣad* asserts that the "Fourth" (*Turīya*) is not a fourth temporal state succeeding the other three, but rather the foundational, unconditioned Reality underlying all three states. The phrase *sa ātmā* establishes that this non-dual Reality (*advaita*) is none other than the true Self (*Ātman*), and *sa vijñeyaḥ* emphasizes that realizing this Self is the supreme goal of knowledge.

### Alternative translations and interpretations

Translators and scholars have rendered *sa ātmā sa vijñeyaḥ* with minor stylistic variations while retaining its core ontological identity:

- **Swami Nikhilananda**: "That is the Self; That is to be known."
- **S. Radhakrishnan**: "That is the Self, That is to be known."
- **F. Max Müller**: "He is the Self, he is to be known."

### Distinction between Upanishadic wording and later commentaries

The brief prose text of the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* sets forth the core doctrine of the four quarters (*pāda*) of the Self and the symbolic identity with *AUM*. Subsequent commentaries and treatises elaborated upon the term *sa ātmā* to address specific philosophical questions:

1. **Gauḍapāda's *Kārikā* (*Āgama Prakaraṇa*):** Gauḍapāda clarifies that *Turīya* is the non-dual Lord (*advaitaḥ sarva-bhāvānām*) who pervades all existences while remaining free from the bondage of cause and effect (*kārya-kāraṇa-baddha*). While *Viśva* and *Taijasa* are bound by both cause and effect, and *Prājña* is bound by cause alone, *Turīya*, designated by *sa ātmā*, is untouched by causality or illusion.
2. **Śaṅkarācārya's commentary (*Māṇḍūkyopaniṣad-bhāṣya*):** Śaṅkara emphasizes that the phrase *sa ātmā* establishes the method of superimposition and negation (*adhyāropa-apavāda*). Śaṅkara notes that the negation of attributes (*nāntaḥ-prajñam...*) does not yield a void (*śūnya*); rather, it establishes the positive, self-luminous reality of the Self (*Turīya*). Just as negating the false appearance of a snake reveals the true nature of the underlying rope, the negation of the attributes of the three states through *sa ātmā sa vijñeyaḥ* reveals the non-dual Self as the sole abiding Reality.

### Advaita Vedānta interpretation

In later Advaita Vedānta literature (such as Sadananda's *Vedāntasāra* and Vidyāraṇya's *Pañcadaśī*), the expression *sa ātmā* in verse 7 of the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* is treated alongside the Great Sayings (*mahāvākya*), specifically *Ayam Ātmā Brahma* ("This Self is Brahman") from verse 2. Advaita scholars utilize *sa ātmā* to demonstrate *akhaṇḍārtha*—the direct, indivisible identity of the individual conscious witness (*sākṣin*) with the Supreme Non-dual Reality (*nirguṇa-brahman*), freed from all limiting adjuncts (*upādhi*).
